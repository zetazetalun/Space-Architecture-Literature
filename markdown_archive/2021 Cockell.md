## Abstract
As humans explore and settle in space, they will need to mine elements to support industries such as manufacturing and construction. In preparation for the establishment of permanent human settlements across the Solar System, we conducted the ESA BioRock experiment on board the International Space Station to investigate whether biological mining could be accomplished under extraterrestrial gravity conditions. We tested the hypothesis that the gravity (g) level influenced the efficacy with which biomining could be achieved from basalt, an abundant material on the Moon and Mars, by quantifying bioleaching by three different microorganisms under microgravity, simulated Mars and Earth gravitational conditions. 

## Key Findings
- *Sphingomonas desiccabilis* and *Bacillus subtilis* significantly enhanced vanadium leaching from basalt compared to sterile controls.
- Gravity conditions (microgravity, simulated Mars, and Earth gravity) did not significantly influence the mean leaching efficacy.
- Bioleaching can achieve an approximate two-fold increase in vanadium leaching over non-biological processes in space environments.

## Methods
- **Microorganisms:** *Sphingomonas desiccabilis* CP1D, *Bacillus subtilis* NCIB 3610, and *Cupriavidus metallidurans* CH34.
- **Substrate:** Olivine basalt rock collected from Iceland (Moon/Mars analog).
- **Hardware:** ESA BioRock apparatus containing Experiment Units (EU) with culture medium reservoirs and gas-permeable membranes.
- **Experiment Duration:** 21 days on the International Space Station (ISS).

## Conclusion
The results demonstrate the potential for biological vanadium sequestration under Martian and asteroidal gravity conditions. This demonstrates that microbial cellular metabolism remains functional in diverse gravity environments, enabling the use of bioreactors for ISRU, bioremediation, and recycling to support long-term human presence and extraterrestrial construction.
