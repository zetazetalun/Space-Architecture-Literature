# Earthmoving for the extraterrestrial

**Author:** Jeffrey S Nesbit (Temple University, USA)  
**Published in:** *EPD: Society and Space*, 2025, Vol. 43(2) 245–261  
**DOI:** 10.1177/02637758231204701

## Abstract
The United States spaceport, and more importantly, its technical landscape, operate in the background for the technological and political progress in pursuit of the extraterrestrial. Throughout the construction of the launch complexes on the Florida coastline, earthmoving became standard practice to elevate rocket pads above sea level and protect against rocket blasts. However, a more extended history of earthmoving at Cape Canaveral is necessary. From Earth’s early geological formations and indigenous burial mounds to the modernization of rockets, Cape Canaveral presents itself as an evolution of terrestrial form. And still, earthmoving continues today. In 2016, an article advised the greatest threats to NASA’s landscape are rising sea levels and hurricanes, causing substantial erosion to the beach, leaving active and historically significant launch facilities at risk. Cape Canaveral beaches are now preparing for additional dredging, importing new soil, and raising beach and dune elevations. This article reveals a critical history of place-based science on Cape Canaveral through an evolution of earthmoving practices, from cultural commemoration, extraterrestrial imagination, and contemporary environmental crises.

## Construction History and Earthmoving Techniques
In 1950, the US Air Force selected the Central Florida eastern coastline to construct missile operations and rocket launch facilities. The construction required significant modification of the natural landscape:

*   **Dredging and Hydraulic Fill:** Engineers dredged hydraulic fill from the Banana River and pumped it into construction sites to elevate rocket launch pads above sea level.
*   **Sandpiles:** For the Saturn V (Apollo) launch pads, two large pyramid-like sandpiles were constructed as foundations.
*   **Mining Equipment Adaptation:** NASA adapted steam-shovel crawlers (originally used for surface coal mining in Kentucky) to construct the Missile Crawler Transporter Facility and the transport vehicles moving rockets from the VAB to the pad.
*   **Culverts and Hydrology:** Massive subterranean water channels (culverts) were engineered to allow the crawler way to traverse Banana Creek, though this subsequently disrupted regional water flows.

## Archaeological Context
Before modern aerospace infrastructure, the region was modified by indigenous groups (Miccosukee, Seminole, Ais) who created mounds:
*   **Burial Mounds:** Sculpted earth reaching 25 feet high and 100 feet across.
*   **Turtle Mound:** A shell midden formed from oysters by the Timucua people.

## Conclusion
The evolution of launch site infrastructure is a way to decipher the evolutions of territory. Earthmoving practices in the militarized infrastructural launch complex leave a stratification of ground and a fragmented history. Today, climate change is forcing a return to these earthmoving remedies to protect active and historically significant launch facilities from beach erosion and inundation.
