# Exploiting biocementation for Martian construction: pathways, challenges, and research gaps

**Author:** Shiva Khoshtinat
**Affiliation:** Politecnico di Milano, Department of Chemistry, Materials and Chemical Engineering "Giulio Natta", Milan, Italy
**Journal:** Advances in Space Research 77 (2026) 604–634
**Published:** Available online 20 October 2025

## Abstract
As ambitions for sustained human presence on Mars accelerate, in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) becomes essential for overcoming the logistical and economic barriers of transporting construction materials from Earth. Biocementation via Microbially Induced Calcium carbonate Precipitation (MICP) offers a promising ISRU-compatible alternative, operating under low-energy conditions suitable for Mars’ constrained power infrastructure. This review critically assesses the potential of biocementation for Martian construction by examining microbial pathways, Martian regolith chemistry, water availability, and environmental stressors such as radiation, low pressure, and temperature fluctuations. A comparative analysis of Martian regolith simulants and their compatibility with MICP is presented, alongside an evaluation of microbial viability under Martian conditions. Despite growing interest, the literature remains sparse and fragmented, lacking empirical data under relevant Martian analogue conditions. This review identifies seven key research gaps and proposes a strategic roadmap to address these challenges.

## 1. Introduction
The aspiration for long-term extraterrestrial habitation highlights the critical importance of ISRU. Transport costs are estimated at $5,000–$20,000 per kilogram to the Moon and are expected to be significantly higher for Mars. Martian regolith, primarily silicates, oxides, and perchlorates, presents a medium for construction. Traditional methods like thermal sintering (~1372 MJ/tonne) are energy-intensive. Biocementation (~29.3 MJ/tonne) offers a potentially transformative, energy-efficient approach.

## 2. Biocementation Pathways
Biocementation leverages microbial metabolism to precipitate calcium carbonate ($CaCO_3$). The study evaluates six primary pathways:

1.  **Ureolysis:** Urea hydrolysis, the most studied pathway. High reaction kinetics and efficiency.
2.  **Ammonification:** Breakdown of amino acids; slower than ureolysis.
3.  **Photosynthesis:** $CO_2$ fixation by cyanobacteria/microalgae; produces oxygen as a byproduct.
4.  **Denitrification:** Anaerobic respiration using nitrate; independent of light, suitable for underground habitats.
5.  **Sulphate Reduction:** Utilizes widespread Martian sulphates (20–40% in some regions); anaerobic.
6.  **Methanogenesis:** $CO_2$ utilization with methane production; synergistic with fuel ISRU.

## 3. Martian Regolith and Resources
Martian regolith contains approximately 5.7% to 6.7% Calcium Oxide (CaO), essential for MICP. However, simulant compositions vary significantly:
*   **Exolith Simulants:** MGS-1, MGS-1C, MGS-1S, JEZ-1.
*   **NASA Simulants:** MMS-1, MMS-2, JSC Mars-1.

## 5. Strategic Roadmap and Research Gaps
The review identifies seven core research gaps:
1.  **Knowledge Gap:** Lack of integrated interdisciplinary overviews.
2.  **Theoretical Gap:** Absence of robust underlying models for extraterrestrial conditions.
3.  **Empirical Gap:** Need for controlled experiments replicating Mars factors (gravity, UV, pressure).
4.  **Evidence Gap:** Sparse data on long-term durability under thermal cycling.
5.  **Population Gap:** Limited diversity of microbial species explored.
6.  **Methodological Gap:** Need for standardized, Mars-optimized protocols.
7.  **Practical-Knowledge Gap:** Disconnect between lab results and mission-relevant scales.

Proposed solution: Use of pressurized, UV-shielded geodesic domes for initial construction phases to mitigate environmental stressors while maintaining microbial viability.
