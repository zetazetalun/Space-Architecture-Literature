# Exploiting biocementation for Martian construction: pathways, challenges, and research gaps

**Author:** Shiva Khoshtinat
**Affiliation:** Politecnico di Milano, Department of Chemistry, Materials and Chemical Engineering “Giulio Natta”, Milan, Italy
**Journal:** Advances in Space Research 77 (2026) 604–634
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.asr.2025.10.061

## Abstract
As ambitions for sustained human presence on Mars accelerate, in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) becomes essential. Biocementation via Microbially Induced Calcium carbonate Precipitation (MICP) offers a promising ISRU-compatible alternative, operating under low-energy conditions. This review critically assesses the potential of biocementation for Martian construction by examining microbial pathways, Martian regolith chemistry, water availability, and environmental stressors. It identifies seven key research gaps and proposes a strategic roadmap to address these challenges.

## 1. Introduction
Mars is the most viable candidate for human colonization due to its proximity and ISRU potential. Transporting materials from Earth is cost-prohibitive ($5,000–$20,000/kg to the Moon; significantly more for Mars). Biomineralization offers a transformative approach, allowing for the formation of minerals under mild, energy-efficient conditions.

## 2. Biocementation Pathways
The review evaluates several microbial metabolic processes for inducing mineral precipitation:
- **Ureolysis:** Most extensively studied; fast reaction; produces ammonia as a byproduct.
- **Ammonification of amino acids:** Similar to ureolysis but slower.
- **Photosynthesis:** Sustainable via CO2 utilization; requires sunlight/shielding.
- **Denitrification:** Anaerobic; environmentally compatible for closed loops.
- **Sulphate Reduction:** Utilizes abundant Martian sulphates; produces toxic H2S.
- **Methanogenesis:** Potential synergy with rocket fuel production.

### 3.1 Martian Regolith and Simulants
Martian regolith is primarily composed of silicates, iron, and aluminum oxides. The paper compares various simulants (MGS-1, JEZ-1, MMS-1, JSC Mars-1) and notes that CaO content (essential for MICP) in Mars regolith is low (5.7–6.7%), suggesting a need for Earth-supplied calcium in early phases or advanced extraction.

## 5. Research Gaps and Roadmap
Seven core research gaps identified:
1. **Knowledge Gap:** Lack of integrated interdisciplinary overview.
2. **Theoretical Gap:** Lack of robust underlying theories for extreme environments.
3. **Empirical Gap:** Absence of experimental validation under Martian conditions.
4. **Evidence Gap:** Fragmented data from preliminary studies.
5. **Population Gap:** Limited diversity of microbial species explored.
6. **Methodological Gap:** Need for standardized space-oriented protocols.
7. **Practical-Knowledge Gap:** Disconnect between lab results and field applications.

## 6. Conclusion
Establishing MICP on Mars requires integrating synthetic biology, materials science, and planetary engineering. Initial operations may require pressurized, UV-shielded enclosures (e.g., geodesic domes) to maintain microbial viability.
