# Solar 3D printing of lunar regolith

## Abstract
The authors demonstrated the feasibility of sintering lunar regolith layer-by-layer solely using concentrated sunlight. First trials using actual concentrated sunlight led to an inhomogeneous sintering of individual layers of lunar regolith simulant, due to the significant flux density variations caused by atmospheric fluctuations. Tests focusing Xenon light demonstrated however the concept with the manufacturing of the first solar 3D printed brick made of lunar regolith simulant. The use of artificial light enabled steady illumination conditions over several hours as required to sinter lunar regolith layer-by-layer. Actual compressive strength of the sintered bricks, below 5 MPa, might be currently too low for a direct lunar application. High porosity levels and weak layer-to-layer bonding were observed on the micro-scale, but ways of improving the technology were found by reducing the thermal gradient and the cooling time between consecutive layers, thus showing the potential of the process. Adaptation of the technology on the Moon is presented to illustrate the suitability of solar additive manufacturing on the lunar surface, as a possible technique for shielding habitats and building roads prior to arrival of the astronauts.

## 1. Introduction
In-situ resource utilization (ISRU) can reduce risk and cost of lunar missions. Building elements like roads, launchpads, and habitat shelters can be produced by solidifying lunar soil via sintering—forming a solid mass by heat without reaching liquefaction. Previous additive manufacturing (AM) for lunar regolith (Contour Crafting, D-shape) used terrestrial binders. This work explores using concentrated sunlight as an unlimited energy source for layer-by-layer 3D printing without binders.

## 2. Experimental set-up
- **Solar Furnace (DLR-Köln):** 52 m² heliostat, 147 mirror facets, providing up to 21 kW (peak flux 4 MW/m²).
- **Xenon Solar Simulator:** Ten 21 kW Xenon lamps used for stable, long-term 3D printing experiments. Specifically, two lamps provided 4.1 kW and 1.2 MW/m² flux density on a 20 mm spot.
- **Apparatus:** 3-axis table moving a testbed under a fixed beam, synchronized with a powder deposition system.

## 3. As-received material
Lunar simulants **JSC-1A** and **JSC-2A** (mare soil simulants) were used. These simulants match Apollo samples in mineral composition (anorthite, pyroxene, olivine) and grain size distribution (95% between upper and lower Apollo bounds).

## 4. Solar additive manufacturing
Initial tests at the solar furnace (0D, 1D, 2D) calibrated sintering speed and shutter opening. Significant findings included:
- **Thermal Stress:** Fast cooling at edges led to bending/warping of sintered parts.
- **Warping Mitigation:** Minimized by alternate scanning patterns and using a porous ceramic substrate to bond the first layers.
- **Scanning Speed:** 10 mm/s to 50 mm/s were tested. Faster speeds (e.g., 48 mm/s) required optimization of light flux density.

## 5. Analysis and discussion

- **Microstructure:** Tomography revealed 0.20 to 0.60 mm thick sintered layers. Open pores up to 0.60 mm indicated poor bonding between layers. High volume fraction of melt was confirmed by SEM.
- **Optimization:** Bonding was improved by reducing the deposition interval between layers (from 60s to 20s), maintaining higher residual temperatures (~525 °C) to facilitate diffusion.

## 7. Conclusion
The study successfully demonstrated the feasibility of 3D printing brick-shaped parts from regolith in ~5 hours. Current strength (2.3 MPa) is lower than traditional sintering (150 MPa) but can be improved by reducing thermal gradients and potentially operating in a vacuum to prevent oxidation and improve heat distribution.
