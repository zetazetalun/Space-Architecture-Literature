# Regolith sintering and 3D printing for lunar construction: An extensive review on recent progress

**Authors:** Muhammad Shazwan Suhaizan, Phuong Tran, Ash Exner, Brian G. Falzon  
**Published in:** Progress in Additive Manufacturing (2024) 9:1715–1736  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s40964-023-00537-1

## Abstract
In-Situ Resource Utilisation (ISRU) is increasingly being seen as a viable and essential approach to constructing infrastructure for human habitation on the moon. This review presents a comprehensive study on the development of several sintering techniques of lunar regolith simulant to demonstrate its feasibility for ISRU on the moon. Various critical processing parameters are evaluated in pursuit of creating a structural material that can withstand the extreme lunar environment. Finally, current challenges are addressed and potential improvements suggested.

## 1. Background of Lunar Construction
Building habitats on the Moon's South Pole is a priority due to ice deposits. However, transport costs from Earth (approx. $3k/kg via SpaceX Falcon 9) make ISRU essential. Regolith sintering is a method to consolidate loose powder into solid structural elements using high temperatures.

## 2. Regolith Simulants and Soils
Lunar regolith is a clastic material consisting of >40% silicon, with varying portions of Al, Ti, and Fe. Simulants like JSC-1A, NU-LHT, and LHS-1 are used to replicate these properties for engineering trials.

## 3. Bulk Sintering Techniques
### 3.2 Radiant Heat Sintering
Involves using heating elements to irradiate regolith. Sintering temperatures typically range from 1100 to 1200°C for mare simulants.
*   **Strength:** 30–200 MPa.
*   **Porosity:** Below 1% in some trials with high pre-pressing.

### 3.3 Microwave Sintering
Uses electromagnetic radiation (300 MHz-300 GHz). Lunar soil couples well with microwave radiation, particularly mare soils with higher FeO and TiO2.
*   **Strength:** 10–50 MPa.
*   **Pros:** Energy efficient; can reach depth.
*   **Cons:** Risk of thermal cracking due to uneven heating.

### 3.4 Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS)
Combines pulsed electric current, external pressure, and rapid heating in a vacuum.
*   **Performance:** Achieved 98% density at 700°C under 50 MPa pressure.
*   **Hardness:** Recorded up to 8.51 GPa for FJS-1 simulant.

## 4. Focused Sintering
### 4.1 Laser Sintering
Rapidly heats a small volume using high-power lasers (~1070 nm). Ideal scan speeds range from 25 to 250 mm/s.
*   **Strength:** Up to 30 MPa (with 35% porosity).
*   **Application:** Precision parts and tools.

### 4.2 Solar Sintering
Uses concentrated sunlight via heliostats and lenses. Experiments at DLR used Xenon arc lamps to simulate solar flux.
*   **Strength:** ~2.31 MPa (RegoLight project).
*   **Application:** Bulk shielding and interlocking bricks.

## 5. Additive Manufacturing (AM) Applications
*   **3D Laser Sintering:** High precision, energy intensive.
*   **3D Solar Sintering:** Minimal power but requires direct sunlight and dust management.
*   **Direct Ink Writing (DIW):** Uses regolith-based pastes with polymer binders. Achieved strengths of 1-19 MPa.

## 6. Conclusion
AM technologies are critical for lunar development. While DIW is suitable for large structures, it is limited in strength and requires Earth-delivered binders. Sintering methods (Radiant, Solar, Microwave) offer better ISRU potential but require optimization for microgravity and extreme temperature gradients.
