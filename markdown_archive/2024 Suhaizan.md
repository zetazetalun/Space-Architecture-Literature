# Regolith sintering and 3D printing for lunar construction: An extensive review on recent progress

**Authors:** Muhammad Shazwan Suhaizan, Phuong Tran, Ash Exner, Brian G. Falzon
**Published:** 25 November 2023 (Journal: Progress in Additive Manufacturing, 2024, 9:1715–1736)
**DOI:** 10.1007/s40964-023-00537-1

## Abstract
In-Situ Resource Utilisation (ISRU) is seen as a viable and essential approach to constructing infrastructure for human habitation on the Moon. Transporting materials from Earth is prohibitively expensive. This review presents a comprehensive study on the development of several sintering techniques of lunar regolith simulant to demonstrate its feasibility for ISRU. Various critical processing parameters are evaluated to create structural materials capable of withstanding extreme lunar environments. 

## 1. Background of Lunar Construction
Building a safe and secure human habitat on the Moon, particularly at the South Pole, requires shielding from extreme temperature fluctuations (-248 °C to 77 °C), solar/cosmic radiation, micrometeoroid impacts, and lunar dust. Sintering—consolidating loose powder into a solid material using heat—is a primary solution for structural requirements using lunar soil (regolith).

## 2. Regolith Simulants and Soils
### 2.1 Characteristics of Regolith
- **Composition:** >40% silicon, with portions of titanium, aluminum, and iron.
- **Density:** Bulk density of 1.5–1.66 g/cm³.
- **Thermal Conductivity:** 0.001 W/mK under vacuum.
- **Melting Point:** Typically between 1100 and 1380 °C.

### 2.2 Simulant Development
Common simulants include JSC-1 (mare), NU-LHT (highlands), LHS-1, and LMS-1. Simulants are used to validate experimental trials, though they are limited by the current understanding of actual lunar soil.

## 3. Bulk Sintering
### 3.1 Radiant Heat Sintering
- **Mechanism:** Uses heating elements to irradiate a large volume.
- **Performance:** Achieved compressive strengths of 30–200+ MPa. Highest strength (232 MPa) achieved with fine particle sizes (<212 µm) and high pre-pressing forces.
- **Process Window:** Ideal sintering at ~25 °C below the melting point.

### 3.2 Microwave Sintering
- **Mechanism:** Uses electromagnetic radiation (300 MHz–300 GHz).
- **Performance:** Compressive strengths in the range of 10–50 MPa. Susceptors (e.g., ilmenite) improve heating uniformity.
- **Challenges:** Risk of thermal runaway and cracking due to non-uniform heating.

### 3.3 Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS)
- **Mechanism:** Combines pulsed electric current and external pressure in a vacuum.
- **Performance:** Achieved high densities (up to 2.9 g/cm³) at relatively lower temperatures (900–1050 °C).

## 4. Focused Sintering
### 4.1 Laser Sintering
- **Mechanism:** Rapid heating of a small volume using a high-power laser.
- **Performance:** Achieved compressive strengths up to 30 MPa. Higher laser power increases stiffness but can sacrifice print quality.

### 4.2 Solar Sintering
- **Mechanism:** Focusing sunlight to heat regolith directly.
- **Performance:** Low mechanical strength (e.g., 2.31 MPa) but highly sustainable as it uses renewable energy.
- **Project Example:** RegoLight (ESA/DLR) investigated building lunar habitations using solar sintering.

## 5. Additive Manufacturing Applications
- **Direct Ink Writing (DIW):** Uses regolith mixed with organic binders to create paste. Compressive strengths of micro-trusses ranged from 1 to 19 MPa.
- **Comparison:** Laser and solar sintering provide high geometric accuracy for precision parts, while DIW is suited for larger architectural constructions despite limited mechanical strength.

## 6. Conclusion
Exploiting local materials (ISRU) is the most pragmatic approach to lunar construction. Future work needs to verify high-temperature behavior in actual lunar conditions and develop non-destructive testing methods for quality control of manufactured structures.
