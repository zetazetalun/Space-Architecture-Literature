# Fiber-reinforced concrete for complex engineering conditions: material properties, nonlinear response and sustainable applications in spaceflight

**Authors:** Junyu Liu and Xi Liu*
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1515/htmp-2025-0102
**Published:** January 16, 2026

## Abstract
As space missions evolve toward longer durations and deeper missions, structural materials face unprecedented challenges in extreme extraterrestrial environments, such as extreme temperature swings, intense radiation, cyclic loading, and crack propagation. Fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC) has gained increasing attention for its ability to enhance material ductility, energy dissipation, and structural resilience under nonlinear conditions. This review summarizes recent research on FRC, focusing on its mechanical properties, nonlinear response mechanisms, and potential for sustainable construction. The article proposes that future research should focus on FRCs in complex nonlinear service environments (including high strain rates and low gravity in aerospace), providing theoretical support for building the next generation of durable, lightweight, and sustainable aerospace infrastructure.

## 1 Introduction
Concrete is the most widely used building material globally. With increasing needs for environmentally conscious progress, the recycling and reusing of construction solid waste are crucial strategies. Waste materials can be utilized as filler, fibers, or substitutes for fine aggregate in concrete. In aerospace infrastructure, models are being developed to resist large temperature differences and harsh environments, generating digital aggregates that match real-world counterparts to characterize mechanical characteristics.

## 2 Fibers
Fibers are categorized into two types: natural and synthetic. These are provided as either short/discontinuous strands (5 to 50 mm) or uninterrupted filaments.

### 2.1 Natural fibers
Natural fibers like straw, hemp, and kenaf exhibit decreased mass-to-volume ratios and improved shock absorption. Research shows that integrating plant-derived fibers significantly enhances structural integrity and mitigates shrinkage.

**Table 1: Advantages and disadvantages of natural fibers.**
| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| :--- | :--- |
| Low density | Low strength |
| Exceptional wear resistance | Highly hygroscopic |
| High toughness | Flammable |
| Outstanding strength relative to weight | Restricted thermal processing range (200–230 °C) |
| Biodegradable | Prone to fungal and microbial attack |
| Shrinkage resistance | Low durability |
| Bridging in cracks | - |
| Insulation | - |

### 2.2 Synthetic fibers
Synthetic fibers (carbon, aramid, glass, basalt) can significantly reduce initial cracks; adding specific volume fractions can decrease the nominal total area of cracks by up to 96.8%.

**Table 2: Advantages and disadvantages of synthetic fibers.**
| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| :--- | :--- |
| Exceptional load-bearing capacity and structural integrity | Non-biodegradable |
| Moisture resistant | Not environment friendly |
| More durable than natural fibers | Poor insulation capacity |
| Avoid reliance on cultivated crops | Higher density compared to natural fiber |
| Shrinkless | Component weight increases |
| Rapid drying | - |
| Superior deformation tolerance and abrasion resilience | - |

Advanced manufacturing techniques like 3D printing of polymer materials combined with microwave heating technology for post-processing are identified as key directions for optimization in aerospace and other important fields.
