# Review of In Situ Resource Utilization-Based Biocementation and Regolith Consolidation Techniques for Space Applications

**Authors:** Zhen Yan and Satoru Kawasaki  
**Published in:** *Buildings* 2025, 15, 1815  
**DOI:** 10.3390/buildings15111815

## Abstract
With the advancement of space exploration, sustainable construction technologies using In Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) have become essential. This review analyzes ISRU-based construction technologies, emphasizing biocementation techniques, their challenges in extreme environments, and prospective research directions.

## 1. Introduction
Establishing extraterrestrial human settlements requires reducing dependency on Earth-sourced materials due to logistical and financial challenges. ISRU strategies focus on converting local resources from celestial bodies like the Moon and Mars into infrastructure and life-supporting resources.

## 2. Overview of Current Construction Technologies Based on ISRU

### 2.1. Sulfur Concrete
- **Mechanism:** Sulfur melts at 120 °C and crystallizes upon cooling to bond regolith aggregates.
- **Performance:** Martian formulations (50% sulfur, 50% simulant) achieve compressive strengths >50 MPa.
- **Challenges:** Sublimation in vacuum (especially at higher temperatures) and volume contraction (up to 13%) during cooling causing internal stress.

### 2.2. Polymer Concrete
- **Mechanism:** Uses polymers (e.g., polyethylene) as binders for regolith.
- **Performance:** 10% polymer content results in 12.6–12.9 MPa compressive strength. Lower binder contents (2–5 wt%) can still produce high flexural strength.
- **Challenges:** Thermal degradation during lunar day/night cycles and the need to transport polymers from Earth (unless synthesized in situ).

### 2.3. Geopolymer Concrete
- **Mechanism:** Three-stage geosynthesis (alkali activation, reorientation, polycondensation).
- **Performance:** Volcanic ash-based geopolymers reached ~45 MPa strength even after freeze-thaw cycles.
- **Challenges:** High iron content in Martian soil and CO2 carbonation can reduce strength.

### 2.4. Sintering
- **Mechanism:** Compaction and heating (sunlight, microwave, or laser) below the melting point (50–70%).
- **Performance:** Solar sintering can create interlocking bricks (0.1 mm layers) in ~5 hours.
- **Challenges:** High energy consumption (lasers/microwaves) and maintenance of solar concentrators in dusty environments.

## 3. Current Research Status of Space Biocement
Biocementation via Microbially Induced Carbonate Precipitation (MICP) or Enzyme-Induced Carbonate Precipitation (EICP) uses urease pathways to precipitate calcium carbonate (CaCO3) as a binder.

- **Key Advances:** 
  - Integration of guar gum and nickel enhances MICP strength.
  - Microalga *Thraustochytrium striatum* can induce biocementation using calcium acetate.
  - Biocarbonation using MgO achieved ~10 MPa strength.
  - Soybean-derived EICP remains effective in microgravity.
- **Waste Utilization:** Use of human urine as a urea source and recycled organic waste for calcium sources.

## 4. Future Directions
Future research must address:
- Water requirements and ice extraction.
- Microbial/enzymatic stability under radiation and temperature extremes.
- Injection methods for grouting in microgravity.
- Closed-loop urine processing for urea supply.

## 5. Conclusions
ISRU construction technologies are vital for establishing extraterrestrial habitats. While biocementation is promising due to its low energy requirements, interdisciplinary collaboration is needed to overcome environmental challenges like low gravity and high radiation.
