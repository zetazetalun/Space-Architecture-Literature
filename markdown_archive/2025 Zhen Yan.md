# Review of In Situ Resource Utilization-Based Biocementation and Regolith Consolidation Techniques for Space Applications

**Authors:** Zhen Yan, Satoru Kawasaki
**Journal:** Buildings 2025, 15, 1815
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings15111815
**Published:** 25 May 2025

## Abstract
With the advancement of space exploration, the development of sustainable construction technologies has become essential for the establishment of enduring extraterrestrial habitats. In Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) assumes a pivotal role by facilitating the use of indigenous materials on celestial bodies such as the Moon and Mars, thereby reducing reliance on terrestrial resources. This review provides a comprehensive analysis of the latest ISRU-based construction technologies, with particular emphasis on biocementation techniques. It further examines the challenges associated with the application of biocementation in extreme space environments and outlines prospective research directions.

## 1. Introduction
Space agencies are developing plans for lunar bases and Martian colonies. The primary obstacle is the logistical challenge of transporting construction materials from Earth. ISRU involves the extraction and conversion of local resources (regolith) into building materials, life-support resources, and fuel.

## 2. Overview of Current Construction Technologies Based on ISRU

### 2.1. Sulfur Concrete
- **Mechanism:** Sulfur melts at 120°C, transitions to monoclinic crystalline form at 114°C, and becomes orthorhombic upon further cooling.
- **Strengths:** Martian concrete (50% sulfur, 50% simulant) achieved compressive strengths exceeding 50 MPa. Microwave-cast Martian concrete using magnetite reached 17.44 MPa.
- **Challenges:** Sublimation in vacuum (especially above -60°C) and shrinkage (up to 13% total volume reduction) lead to internal stresses and defects.

### 2.2. Polymer Concrete
- **Mechanism:** Uses polymers (e.g., polyethylene) as binders for regolith aggregates.
- **Performance:** 10% polyethylene with 90% lunar soil achieved 12.6–12.9 MPa. Solar energy can be used to melt recycled polyethylene powder for brick production (5% polymer = 1-2 MPa; 10% = 3-8 MPa).
- **Radiation Shielding:** Polyethylene-regolith composites (40 wt% polyethylene) show effective radiation attenuation and impact resistance.

### 2.3. Geopolymer Concrete
- **Mechanism:** Alkali activation of aluminosilicates found in regolith. 
- **Performance:** Volcanic ash-based geopolymers (simulating lunar regolith) reached 45.53 MPa under vacuum. Water recovery rates of up to 98.61% are possible via condensation systems.
- **Mars Constraints:** High iron content and CO2 atmosphere (carbonation) may reduce strength.

### 2.4. Sintering
- **Methods:** Solar, microwave, or laser sintering.
- **Process:** Solar furnaces can sinter 0.1 mm layers to create interlocking components in ~5 hours. These can protect inflatable habitats from radiation and micrometeoroids.

## 3. Current Research Status of Space Biocement
- **Technologies:** Microbially Induced Carbonate Precipitation (MICP) and Enzyme-Induced Carbonate Precipitation (EICP).
- **Progress:** *Sporosarcina pasteurii* can consolidate regolith into bricks. Adding 1% guar gum and 10 mM nickel enhances strength. 
- **Sustainability:** New methods use biocarbonation with MgO (~10 MPa strength) or calcium phosphate precipitation from kitchen waste (soybean enzymes and bone minerals), which remains effective in microgravity.

## 5. Conclusions
ISRU technologies are vital for reducing dependency on Earth. While thermal and chemical methods (sintering, sulfur concrete) are advanced, biocementation offers a low-energy, sustainable path, provided challenges regarding water, urea sourcing (urine), and microbial stability in extreme environments are addressed.
