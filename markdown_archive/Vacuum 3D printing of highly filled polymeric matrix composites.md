# Vacuum 3D printing of highly filled polymeric matrix composites

**Authors:** Emanuele Alberto Slejko, Nicholas Sesto Gorella, Advenit Makaya, Paolo Gallina, Nicola Scuor, Stefano Seriani
**Journal:** Acta Astronautica 204 (2023) 25–33
**Publisher:** Elsevier

## ABSTRACT
In this contribution, we have investigated how polymeric matrix composites for aerospace applications are affected, when fabricated at low pressure, by the additive manufacturing process. Commercial filaments have been selected based on their representativeness of materials derived from lunar resources. Standard samples of thermoplastic polymers reinforced by organic fibers and inorganic fillers have been printed inside a vacuum chamber, and their mechanical and thermal properties have been characterized and discussed based on the printing conditions. The reported study represents a preliminary investigation of the potential applicability of the 3D printing technology on highly filled polymers for extraterrestrial applications.

## 1. Introduction
Vacuum 3D printing represents an evolution of the typical additive manufacturing process, which is usually conducted in atmosphere and standard pressure. Future exploration and colonization missions will require the capabilities to produce structures and mechanisms directly in-orbit or on-planet. Being able to print directly in vacuum has at least two advantages: 1) it forgoes the requirement of having a pressurized environment; 2) it enables very large structures to be printed. 

The addition of In Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) is considered a critical part of the manufacturing process. The challenge relies not only in being able to print under vacuum but recurring to a high portion of raw material already present in the target environment (the Moon, Mars, etc.). 3D printed composites based on polymeric material present the advantage of being easily processable and manufacturable in complex geometries.

## 2. Materials and methods
### 2.1. Materials
Three commercial filaments were used: PLA, PLA filled with 40% organic fibers (pine wood fibers) and PLA filled with 50% inorganic clays. All filaments are PLA-based, with the addition of an impact modifier. These highly filled filaments were selected as representatives of plant-based materials reinforced with large amounts of lunar resources.

### 2.3 & 2.4. 3D printer specifications and adaptation
The Monoprice Select Mini V2 was chosen and modified to fit into a vacuum chamber (pressure as low as 10^-4 bar). The extruder motor was relocated, the cooling fan was replaced with a conductive-type cooling system (copper braids), and the control board was moved outside the chamber.

## 3. Results and discussion
### 3.1. Representativeness of starting materials
The inorganic clay powder mainly consists of CaSiO3, highly representative of chemical compositions processed from the lunar environment. The pine wood filler is representative of organic material that could be grown in lunar environments (e.g., similar to cotton stalks grown during the Chang'e-4 mission).

### 3.2. Characterization of samples
Comparison of the mechanical properties indicates no major differences between atmospheric pressure (1 bar) and low-pressure (10^-4 bar) printed samples. FTIR spectra showed no sensible difference in the fingerprint region, meaning the chemical composition is preserved irrespective of printing pressure.
Thermal characterization (DSC) showed that the total crystallinity of the samples did not substantially change under vacuum. Since layer thickness was thin (0.175 mm), the heat transfer is not sensibly influenced by the reduction of convection when printing under low pressure.
Dimensional accuracy of printed parts is strongly influenced by printing conditions. Samples present a thinner but wider neck when printed under low pressure, speculated to result from a larger surface tension of the molten strand in a vacuum environment.

## 4. Conclusions
We have demonstrated that 3D printing can be conducted in a vacuum chamber with appropriate thermal control. Two different commercial highly filled PLA filaments have been printed at pressure as low as 10^-4 bar; the quality of the material is not affected in terms of mechanical and thermal properties. Instead, dimensional deviations have been observed for low pressure printed parts due to higher surface tension of extruded strands. This lack of convection does not affect the quality of the part as long as the cooling rate is high.
