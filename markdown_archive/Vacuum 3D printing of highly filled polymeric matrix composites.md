# Vacuum 3D printing of highly filled polymeric matrix composites

**Journal:** Acta Astronautica 204 (2023) 25–33  
**Authors:** Emanuele Alberto Slejko, Nicholas Sesto Gorella, Advenit Makaya, Paolo Gallina, Nicola Scuor, Stefano Seriani

## Abstract
In this contribution, we have investigated how polymeric matrix composites for aerospace applications are affected, when fabricated at low pressure, by the additive manufacturing process. Commercial filaments have been selected based on their representativeness of materials derived from lunar resources. Standard samples of thermoplastic polymers reinforced by organic fibers and inorganic fillers have been printed inside a vacuum chamber, and their mechanical and thermal properties have been characterized and discussed based on the printing conditions.

## 1. Introduction
Future exploration and colonization missions will require the capabilities to produce structures and mechanisms directly in-orbit or on-planet. Printing directly in vacuum has at least two advantages: 1) it forgoes the requirement of having a pressurized environment; 2) it enables very large structures to be printed. While 3D printing in microgravity has been demonstrated (e.g., on the ISS), printing in vacuum remains a challenge, particularly when considering In Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU).

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Materials
Three commercial filaments were used: PLA, PLA filled with 40% organic fibers (pine wood), and PLA filled with 50% inorganic clays. These represent plant-based materials and lunar-regolith-like fillers respectively.

### 2.3. 3D Printer Specifications

### 2.4. 3D Printer Adaptation to Vacuum
Modifications included:
1. Relocation of the extruder motor from the top of the printer.
2. Substitution of the cooling fan with copper thermal braids for conductive heat dissipation.
3. External relocation of the control board and interface.
4. Replacement of the print bed sheet with Kapton tape.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1. Mechanical Properties
The mechanical properties of filled materials showed no major differences between atmospheric pressure and low-pressure (10⁻⁴ bar) printed samples. The chemical composition, confirmed via FTIR and XRD, remained preserved during vacuum processing.

### 3.2. Dimensional Accuracy
Samples printed under low pressure presented a thinner but wider neck in dogbone samples. This is attributed to the increased surface tension of the molten strand in vacuum, which enhances wettability on the substrate.

## 4. Conclusions
The study demonstrates that 3D printing can be conducted in vacuum with appropriate thermal control (conduction-based). While mechanical and thermal properties are unaffected, dimensional deviations occur due to surface tension effects, which must be accounted for in design.
