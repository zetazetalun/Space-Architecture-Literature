# A review on design and construction of the lunar launch/landing infrastructure

## Abstract
With advances in automated construction and deeper Moon exploration missions, the concept of “building a house on the moon” is becoming increasingly feasible. The launch/landing (LL) infrastructure plays a vital role in ensuring the safety of landers and astronauts and should be prioritized for construction. Initially, this review explores the variety of designs used at existing LL sites, analyzing the logic and unique features of each design. It then outlines three main construction methods for landing pads, from material production to basic construction processes. The review also covers experiments related to LL pads, including material, component, and construction testing. Finally, current challenges and future directions for the development of lunar LL infrastructure are discussed, and 3D-printed biomimetic structures are highlighted as a particularly promising option.

## 1. Introduction
The Moon serves as humanity's primary gateway to exploring the universe. The safety of launch and landing for space landers is key to accomplishing lunar exploration and construction missions. Unlike on Earth, the Moon lacks established launch and landing facilities, presenting significant safety challenges. Plumes ejected by lander engines cause damage to the lunar surface, and loose regolith can easily form large craters. Moreover, lunar dust is blown and suspended in the air for a long period, interfering with sensing equipment and posing an erosion threat to surrounding infrastructure.

## 2. Design of the lunar landing/launching infrastructure
### 2.1. Launch/landing area
The size of the landing site is determined by the range of lunar regolith particle travel and the landing accuracy of the lander. (Susante and Metzger, 2016) proposed a concentric circle design with inner and outer ring diameters of 12 m and 27 m, respectively. The “Olympus” project proposed total site diameter sizes of 100 m, 75 m, and 50 m.

### 2.2. Launch/landing pad
Basic components include: a central pad, an external ring, a dust trench, and a blast wall. The central pad is designed to withstand direct heat and pressure impacts, while the external ring and dust trench are intended to intercept high-speed ejected particles. Paving and 3D extrusion printing are the two main design approaches.

## 3. In situ construction for lunar launch/landing infrastructure
### 3.1. 3D printing based on concrete extrusion
Extrusion printing involves preprocessing raw materials, manufacturing with auxiliary equipment, and postprocessing. Materials tested include:
- **Polymer concrete**: Mixing lunar soil simulant with thermoplastic powder (e.g., polyethylene) or RTV binders.
- **Sulfur concrete**: Utilizing sulfur as a binder for extraterrestrial applications.
- **Geopolymer concrete**: Based on simulants like JSC-1A and Va.

### 3.2. Sintering, melting and assembly
Techniques include:
- **LASSS (Large Area Surface Sintering System)**: A mobile rover system using resistive heating elements.
- **Solar Sintering**: Utilizing Fresnel lenses or solar concentrators to generate high temperatures (up to 1350 °C).
- **Laser Powder Bed Fusion**: Specifically Selective Separation Sintering (SSS) to produce interlocking tiles.

### 3.3. Building with moon rocks
Rock paving is considered a cost-effective option for stabilizing the peripheral ring (apron) of the landing pad. Sorting and grading rocks by size (armor stone vs. smaller layers) is critical for erosion resistance.

## 5. Challenges and future directions
1. **Multiphysics Modeling**: Difficulty in accurately simulating plume-surface-dust interactions under vacuum/low gravity.
2. **Large-scale Construction**: Issues with material shrinkage and micro-cracking during 3D printing and sintering.
3. **Terrestrial Simulation**: Limits in laboratory environments for combining high heat, vacuum, and low gravity.
4. **Bionic Design**: Promising direction using structures like the "Sunflower Vault" or "Eyelashes" for dust collection and plume diversion.
