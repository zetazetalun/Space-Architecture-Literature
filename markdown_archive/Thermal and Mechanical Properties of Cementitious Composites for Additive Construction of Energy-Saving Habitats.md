# Thermal and Mechanical Properties of Cementitious Composites for Additive Construction of Energy-Saving Habitats

**Authors:** Hongyu Zhou, Adam L. Brooks, Dominic Hanna, and Babak Salarieh  
**Publication:** Earth and Space 2018, ASCE

## Abstract
Lightweight cementitious composites (LWCC) are cement-based materials combined with lightweight fillers (LWF). Their potential for terrestrial and extraterrestrial habitat construction lies in their reduced structural weight and low thermal conductivity. LWCC's small, spherical aggregates make them suitable for extrusion-based 3D printing. This research investigates the effects of different LWFs (hollow glass microspheres and fly ash cenospheres) on mechanical and thermal properties, including high-strain rate performance via split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) tests.

## Introduction
3D printable concrete eliminates formwork, reduces labor costs, and allows for automated, high-precision construction. LWCCs offer advantages in structural weight reduction and energy efficiency due to lower thermal conductivity compared to normal-weight concrete.

## Experimental Program

### Material Preparation and Mixture Design
- **Binder:** ASTM Type I-II Portland cement.
- **Fine Aggregate:** Silica sand.
- **Fillers (LWF):** 
  - Hollow Glass Microspheres (HGM): K25, S38HS, S60.
  - Fly Ash Cenospheres (FAC): E106, E200/600.
- **Water/Cement Ratio:** 0.43.
- **Volume Fractions (vf):** 7%, 14%, 21%, and 28%.

## Results and Discussion

### Compressive Strength
Compressive strength is a function of LWF particle size and shell thickness. While strength generally decreases at high volume fractions (>15%), smaller HGMs with thicker shells (e.g., S60) maintain higher strength than larger, thinner-walled particles (e.g., K25). At 28% volume fraction, S60 mortar was 125% stronger than K25 mortar.

### Thermal Properties
Thermal conductivity is primarily dictated by the volume fraction of LWF rather than the specific type. Increasing volume fraction significantly reduces conductivity (from ~2.3 W/m·K for control to 0.65-0.8 W/m·K at 28% vf).

### Dynamic Behavior (SHPB)
LWCC is strain-rate sensitive. At a strain rate of 400 s⁻¹, dynamic compressive strength was 32.2 MPa (40% higher than quasi-static). Strength increased by up to 66.7% at a strain rate of 920 s⁻¹.

## Conclusions
1. Thermal properties are governed by LWF volume fraction and are insensitive to LWF type.
2. Mechanical properties are heavily influenced by LWF particle size, shell thickness, and crush strength.
3. LWCCs with higher density HGMs (e.g., S60) are suitable for structural load-bearing.
4. Smaller LWF particles distribute better, enhancing suitability for extrusion-based additive construction.
