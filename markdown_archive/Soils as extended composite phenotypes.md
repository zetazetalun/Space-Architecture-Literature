## Abstract
Several recent theories and conceptual frameworks in pedology, ecology, geomorphology, and evolutionary biology suggest the notion that earth’s soils represent selective pressures and expressions of the effects of genes (extended phenotypes). The cumulative effects manifested in the soil represent an extended composite phenotype. This suggests that significant biological changes are reflected in qualitative pedological changes. This is demonstrated via research in the Ouachita Mountains, Arkansas, where selective pressures on trees linked to fire frequency are reflected in soil morphology. This has implications for the search for extraterrestrial life.

## 1. Introduction
Earth's soils are expressions of the cumulative impacts of the biosphere on surface processes. Soils may represent the single best indication of the nature, state, and condition of earth surface systems. If soils are expressions of biological evolution, the implications for understanding the evolution of earth systems are profound.

## 2. Conceptual Framework
### 2.1. Soils as biotic constructs
Van Breemen (1993) argued that soils are influenced by biota to increase net primary productivity. Mechanisms include weathering, increasing nutrient supply, and increasing water-holding capacity.
### 2.2. Biomantles
Soils are increasingly viewed as continuously-mixed biomantles. Biomechanical activities can result in differentiation rather than homogenization of soil layers.
### 2.3. Extended phenotypes and niche construction
Dawkins (1982) introduced the extended phenotype, referring to all effects of a gene on the environment. Ecosystem engineers (Jones et al.) are organisms that have a disproportionate influence on the environment relative to their numbers.

## 3. Paleosols and coevolution
Paleosol evidence shows coevolution of vegetation communities and mammalian fauna. Grassland soils, for instance, show specific morphological properties attributable to vegetation and earthworms over the past 40 Ma.

## 4. Tree–soil interactions
In the Ouachita Mountains, USA, fire frequency is a dominant selective factor. Human manipulation of fire has changed vegetation from hardwood-dominated forests to pine-dominated woodlands, which is reflected in soil morphology.

### 4.4. Pines vs. hardwoods
Pines and hardwoods interact with soils differently:
- **Pines** are first to colonize rock surfaces and initiate weathering.
- **Pines** are more likely to uproot in shallow soils, leading to mounding and biomechanical mixing.
- **Pines** are faster-growing with shorter lifespans, resulting in more rapid turnover of soil features.

## 5. Discussion and conclusions
Soils as extended composite phenotypes imply a tight coupling between the bio- and pedospheres. This ECP framework suggests possibilities for the search for life on other planets. Impact on soil morphology is more long-lasting than signatures in atmospheric chemistry, providing clues about potential extraterrestrial life.
