# Off Earth Landing and Launch Pad Construction—A Critical Technology for Establishing a Long-Term Presence on Extraterrestrial Surfaces\n\n**Authors:** Nathan J. Gelino, Robert P. Mueller, Robert W. Moses, James G. Mantovani, Philip T. Metzger, Brad C. Buckles, and Laurent Sibille\n\n**Abstract:** In 2017, Space Policy Directive–1 aimed to lead human return to the Moon. Long-term presence requires numerous landings/launches, causing erosion and high-velocity ejecta from engine plumes. Construction of reusable landing/launch pad (LLP) infrastructure mitigates these risks by providing a benign surface and protecting assets with blast barriers. This paper baselines the state-of-the-art (SOTA) for LLP construction technologies and establishes comparison metrics for future trade studies.\n\n## Introduction\nTerrestrial vertical landing operations (e.g., SpaceX VTVL) have expanded the infrastructure needed for Launch & Landing Complexes (LLC). Functionally equivalent LLCs must be developed for Moon/Mars missions to ensure safe operations and reasonable life cycle costs. This paper specifically addresses the technologies and methods for constructing the LLP and associated blast barriers.\n\n## Preliminary Engineering Assessment\nLarge-scale landers (Human Landing System - HLS) require 100 kN thrust engines, compared to Apollo’s 16 kN. Research suggests erosion rate scales with thrust/mass; an HLS lander could displace 16 metric tons of regolith, creating ejecta that exceeds lunar escape velocity and damages nearby assets. Novel lander designs (e.g., Hercules) may mitigate this by placing engines at the top of the vehicle.\n\n## State of the Art and Past Work\nThe current Technology Readiness Level (TRL) for LLP construction is approximately TRL-2. Key research examples include:\n- **Sintered Basalt Pavers:** Swamp Works and PISCES (Hawaii, 2016) robotically constructed a pad from sintered basalt pavers. \n- **Soil Stabilization:** Van Susante and Metzger (2016) tested rock beds and pavers; loose regolith was found ineffective.\n- **Lunar Concrete:** A pad using KOHLS-1 simulant and polymer binders was tested for compression strength (Lee, 2012).\n\n## Trade Study Criteria\nTable 1 summarizes criteria across three phases:\n1. **Preparation and Staging Phase:** Up-mass, Difficulty of ISRU handling, Site prep effort, Surface asset reliance, Crew interaction, Robotics/Autonomy, Robustness, TRL, Power.\n2. **Construction Phase:** Constructability, Versatility, Construction time, Verification of as-built performance.\n3. **Operations and Maintenance Phase:** Performance as LLP surface, Expected life, Ease of repairability, Lifecycle cost.\n\n## Landing/Launch Pad Concepts\nTable 2 lists proposed LLP structure concepts:\n- Minimal Preparation / Existing Topography\n- Compacted Regolith / Bedrock / Ice Surface\n- Rock Piles\n- Surface Stabilization (Polymers/Grids)\n- Regolith Bags / Ice Bladders\n- Pavers (Sintered, Polymer-bound, or Solar/Laser joined)\n- Metallic Plates (Imported or Local Alloys)\n- Deployable Structures (Mats/Blankets)\n- Direct Emplacement: Sintered Structures (Solar/Laser), Polymer Concrete, or Hydraulic Concrete Pads.\n\n## Conclusion\nLLP infrastructure is necessary to reduce mission risk and facilitate the utilization of off-Earth resources. This paper provides the baseline for developing these critical technologies as NASA moves toward sustainable lunar exploration by 2028.
