# Off Earth Landing and Launch Pad Construction—A Critical Technology for Establishing a Long-Term Presence on Extraterrestrial Surfaces

**Nathan J. Gelino**; **Robert P. Mueller**; **Robert W. Moses, Ph.D.**; **James G. Mantovani, Ph.D.**; **Philip T. Metzger, Ph.D.**; **Brad C. Buckles**; and **Laurent Sibille, Ph.D.**

## ABSTRACT
In 2017, Space Policy Directive–1 was issued to ensure that “the United States will lead the return of humans to the Moon for long-term exploration and utilization” and engage the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in the national effort. A long-term presence on the Moon will require numerous lunar landings and launches to build up surface assets, rotate crew, and deliver logistics supplies to/from the Moon. Interactions between landing/launch rocket engine plumes and the unprepared lunar surface will lead to erosion and ejection of regolith particles at high velocities from beneath the vehicle. The associated ejecta elevates mission risks by: obscuring sensors and human vision during landing via lofted dust; cratering/modifying the surface that the vehicle will land upon; and subjecting the vehicle, surrounding assets, and potentially orbital assets to impacts and contamination from high-velocity dust particles. The construction of a reusable landing/launch pad infrastructure is a method to mitigate such risks. A landing/launch pad system can provide a known, benign and reliable landing/launch surface, minimize regolith ejecta, and protect surrounding assets from liberated particles. Associated blast barriers can provide protection in case of a landing/launch catastrophic anomaly. This paper summarizes previous work towards construction of off-Earth landing/launch pad infrastructure and begins to identify key technology gaps. Additionally, this paper establishes metrics for comparison of pad construction technologies to guide future trade studies. The overall objective of this paper is to baseline the state of the art of off-Earth landing/launch pad construction technologies and serve as the starting point for further technical development.

## INTRODUCTION
Launch and landing of spacecraft on Earth is a well-defined discipline with over seventy years of operational experience to inform and optimize the subsystems that perform the required functions. When all of these functions are combined into one overall system, the resulting infrastructure is called a Launch & Landing Complex (LLC). Recently, terrestrial vertical landing operations adopted for commercial rocket first stages returning to the launch site ['VTVL', (2020)] has expanded the infrastructure required in a LLC, to include landing pads which mitigate risk by providing a safe and reliable landing zone. Future lunar and Martian spaceports will require mitigations to these launch and landing risks (Benaroya, 2002).

## PRELIMINARY ENGINEERING ASSESSMENT
Large-scale human HLS landers are expected to deliver at least 12 t of payload to the lunar surface and require descent engines with 100 kN of thrust. Analysis of Apollo Lunar Module videos showed that erosion rate scaled as thrust (or lander mass) to the 2.5 power [Lane and Metzger, 2015]. If we assume that the mass erosion rate is approximately proportional to the lander mass, then a HLS lander may displace a total mass of 16 t of lunar regolith during landing on unmodified surfaces. This ejecta will travel at velocities exceeding lunar escape velocity (Lane, 2012) and can cause severe hardware damage to assets (Immer, 2011).

## STATE OF THE ART AND PAST WORK
Although many concepts of LLP construction have been investigated, none have reached a high Technology Readiness Level (overall TRL ~2). Examples include:
*   **Sintered Basalt Pavers:** A VTVL pad was robotically constructed from sintered basalt pavers by Swamp Works and PISCES in Hawaii (Kelso, 2016).
*   **Soil Stabilization:** Van Susante and Metzger (2016) investigated rocks, pavers, and compacted regolith.
*   **Lunar Concrete:** A landing pad made with polymer binders and glass fiber using Korean Lunar Simulants was tested for compression (Lee, 2012).

## TECHNOLOGY GAPS
All concepts remain in a low TRL status. Significant gaps exist in prototyping systems for the lunar environment, specifically regarding thermal vacuums, radiation, reduced gravity, and micrometeoroid impacts.
