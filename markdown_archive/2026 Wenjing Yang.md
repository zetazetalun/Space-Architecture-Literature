# Innovative applications of microbial induced carbonate precipitation (MICP) for extraterrestrial construction

**Authors:** Wenjing Yang, Yuqi Song, P.G. Ranjith, Zhenqian Chen, Hao Fang, Derek Elsworth, Fengshou Zhang, Youzhi Feng, Xiangyang Song

**Journal:** Acta Astronautica 238 (2026) 856–872
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2025.09.051

## Abstract
The idea of in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) for building extraterrestrial bases on the moon and Mars has become increasingly popular. This review outlines the latest developments in fabrication of space construction materials using microbial induced carbonate precipitation (MICP) technology. This innovative approach uses the urease of *Sporosarcina pasteurii* to promote the precipitation of calcium carbonate from external carbon and nitrogen sources. By using lunar and Martian stardust as raw materials, this method presents a sustainable alternative to transporting building materials from Earth. This paper focuses on three key aspects: advancement in understanding lunar and Martian soil simulants, operational mechanisms of MICP, and improvement to the physical properties of space bio-bricks.

## 1. Introduction
A major barrier to lunar base construction is the exorbitant cost of transporting materials from Earth, estimated at ~$90,000 per kilogram. ISRU offers a solution by leveraging local resources like lunar regolith and water ice. Three main types of lunar construction materials have been developed: sintered materials, 3D-printed structures, and bio-bricks.

## 2. MICP feasibility in lunar and Martian surface environments
### 2.1. The feasibility of MICP on lunar surface
The core reactions of MICP—urease-catalyzed urea hydrolysis and calcium carbonate precipitation—require liquid water. Water ice has been confirmed in polar permanently shaded regions (PSRs). TiO2 from ilmenite in lunar soil can also facilitate the production of H2, O2, and H2O via photocatalysis (Technology Readiness Level ≤3).

### 2.2. The feasibility of MICP on Mars surface
Mars atmosphere (95% CO2) and soil (rich in iron oxides and silicates) provide potential resources. Perchlorates in Martian soil inhibit bacterial metabolic activity, but UV radiation can decompose them into low-toxicity chloride ions.

## 4. Research progress of MICP
MICP utilizes urease-producing bacteria (e.g., *Bacillus pasteurii*) to catalyze urea hydrolysis, raising pH and forming carbonate ions ($CO_3^{2-}$) which react with calcium ions ($Ca^{2+}$) to precipitate $CaCO_3$. 

**Key developments:**
- **Additives:** Adding 1% guar gum and 10 mM $NiCl_2$ to regolith simulants achieved a maximum compressive strength of 5.65 MPa.
- **3D Printing:** Biomineral composites capable of withstanding up to 3.5 MPa have been produced.
- **Urine Utilization:** Human urine can serve as a nitrogen/urea source for bio-bricks, yielding compressive strengths around 2.7 MPa.

## 6. Summary and outlook
1. Ilmenite ($TiO_2$) on the Moon can potentially produce hydrogen, oxygen, and water.
2. Additives like guar gum and fibers enhance the mechanical properties of bio-bricks.
3. Research is needed on bacterial adaptation to microgravity and radiation.
4. Future testing of space bricks is planned aboard the Chinese space station (Tianzhou 8).
