# The Research Facility on the Moon\n\n**Mikolaj S. Trus**\nGraduate of the Architecture Studies, Cracow Univ. of Technology, Kraków, Poland.\n\n## ABSTRACT\nThe research facility on the Moon is an experimental, structural design for space exploration. It was developed as the Student Master Thesis at Cracow University of Technology, Faculty of Architecture. The main goal was to introduce innovative extraterrestrial architecture. Modularity, multifunctionality, and simplicity of the concept were key objectives. It was crucial to ensure the safety of the lunar crew. The facility is formed from seven modules. The construction of each of them consists of repetitive, easy to transport components. Project is prepared to host twelve astronauts, but it is expected to expand in the future. This structural concept for lunar architecture is universal and could be useful in other, different circumstances.\n\n## INTRODUCTION\n\n### Concept\nThe idea of a crewed base on the Moon was born at the Cracow University of Technology in February 2019. Difficulties related to transport defined strict requirements regarding the mass, dimensions, and form. The Moon environment presents challenges including 1/6th gravity, lack of atmosphere, meteoroid impacts, solar radiation, and extreme temperature amplitudes.\n\n## METHODS\n1. **Hand drawing:** Focused on visual stimuli and initial concept brainstorming, leading to the selection of the spherical shape as most efficient.\n2. **Virtual model:** Created in Blender 3D to control shape, scale, and modularity. Truncated icosahedrons were identified as the most useful modular unit.\n3. **3D printing:** Used to verify results of virtual design through physical scale models (1:200).\n4. **Technical drawings:** Produced detailed sections and floorplans using CAD to ensure functional interior design.\n\n## RESULTS\n\n### Construction\nThe project utilizes a rigid assembly system over inflatable structures due to higher predictability in altered gravity. The core module is a truncated icosahedron (12 regular pentagons, 20 regular hexagons) which is triangulated for structural integrity. A light rod construction system forms an exoskeleton reinforced with internal support bars. Every module can be divided into twenty repetitive curved triangular elements for transport.\n\n### Protection\nThe protection system consists of a multilayer shell:\n- **Internal layer:** 20 cm thick thermal insulation.\n- **Vacuum layer:** 5 cm gap acting as a thermal insulator.\n- **External layer:** 5 cm thick layer to absorb radiation and temperature differences.\n- **Regolith layer:** The final step involves covering the structure with lunar regolith for radiation absorption and meteoroid protection.\n\n### Function\nThe base consists of seven spherical modules in a circular arrangement (three high, three low, one central). The top modules serve as living areas for four astronauts each. The bottom modules include a dining/kitchen sector, a laboratory sector, and a physical activity/gym sector. The facility supports a crew of twelve.\n\n## DISCUSSION\nThe concept differs from the Foster + Partners lunar project in its assembly method. While the Foster design uses inflatable domes with robotic regolith printing, this concept uses repetitive rigid components and a vacuum-insulated shell before regolith application. Both respond to transport constraints and the need for multi-layered protection.\n\n## CONCLUSION\nThe research proves the potential of regular polyhedrons in extraterrestrial architecture. The structural concept is multipotential and can be adjusted to various environments. Transport issues are resolved through repetitive components, and the functional design remains flexible and adjustable.
