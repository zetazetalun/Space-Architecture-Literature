# Solar 3D printing of lunar regolith

## Abstract
The authors demonstrated the feasibility of sintering lunar regolith layer-by-layer solely using concentrated sunlight. Tests using Xenon light led to the manufacturing of the first solar 3D printed brick made of lunar regolith simulant. Actual compressive strength of the sintered bricks, below 5 MPa, might be currently too low for a direct lunar application. High porosity levels and weak layer-to-layer bonding were observed, but ways of improving the technology were found by reducing the thermal gradient and the cooling time between consecutive layers. Adaptation of the technology on the Moon is presented as a possible technique for shielding habitats and building roads prior to arrival of the astronauts.

## 1. Introduction
In-situ resource utilization (ISRU) can reduce the risk and cost of lunar missions. Engineering elements from lunar soil can create roads, launchpads, and habitat shelters. Sintering is a process of forming a solid mass by heat without reaching the point of liquefaction. Additive manufacturing (AM) technologies, or 3D printing, allow for complex shapes with minimal material waste and no human assistance. Prior approaches like contour crafting or D-shape used binders; this study focuses on pure solar sintering.

## 2. Experimental set-up
Tests were performed at the DLR High-Flux Solar Furnace and the Xenon High-Flux Solar Simulator. The solar oven uses a 52 m² heliostat and a 147-mirror concentrator. The Xenon simulator provided stable conditions with a power of 4.1 kW and a flux density of 1.2 MW/m² focused on a 20 mm spot. A 3-axis table moved the testbed under the beam, synchronized with a powder deposition system.

## 3. Materials
Lunar simulants JSC-1A and JSC-2A (mare soil simulants) were used. JSC-2A contains a glassy phase with a glass transition at 620 °C and melting at 1070 °C.

## 4. Solar Additive Manufacturing
Layers of 0.1 mm thickness were deposited. Sintering speed (3-axis table translational speed) was calibrated against Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI). Successive layers were bonded to form a 3D structure. The study produced bricks of 240 x 120 mm² and 20 x 20 x 20 mm³ cubes.

## 5. Analysis and Discussion
Compression tests showed a strength of 2.31 ± 0.30 MPa and a Young's Modulus of 0.14 ± 0.06 GPa. These values are lower than the 150 MPa achieved by traditional sintering, primarily due to high porosity and weak interlayer bonding. Tomography revealed open pores up to 0.60 mm and closed pores up to 0.45 mm. SEM analysis confirmed that plagioclase minerals remained unmelted at the interface, preventing full fusion.

## 6. Lunar Application
On the Moon, a heliostat could track the sun to provide continuous energy for a brick-factory or a mobile rover equipped with a Fresnel lens to sinter regolith directly. This would provide mass shielding for habitats against radiation and micrometeoroids.

## 7. Conclusion
Solar 3D printing of lunar regolith is feasible. Future improvements include reducing the time between sintering layers to decrease thermal stress, using a vacuum environment to prevent oxidation and improve heat distribution, and increasing the TRL for precursor missions.
