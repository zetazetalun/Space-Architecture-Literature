# Seismic Vulnerability Assessment of Non-Structural Elements Inside an Inflatable Lunar Habitat

**Oscar D. Forero; Julio A. Ramirez, Ph.D.; and Shirley J. Dyke, Ph.D.**

## ABSTRACT
The spirit of exploration and pursuit of knowledge are once again driving humanity to seek a human presence on extraterrestrial bodies. While numerous challenges must be overcome to achieve this goal, the existence of moonquakes has often been overlooked. The Apollo Passive Seismic Experiment (APSE) collected 8 years' worth of data, offering the scientific community insights into the Moon's internal mechanisms. Using NASA's Moon to Mars Architecture requirements for surface habitation, a procedure for vulnerability assessment is developed herein for essential non-structural elements (NSE) inside an inflatable habitat. The NSE element used for this study is the environmental control and life support system (ECLSS). The aim of this study is to propose an approach to assess the vulnerability of the NSE that supports essential equipment under a paucity of information related to the seismic hazard. We also emphasize herein that the launch dynamic environment behaves differently, yielding a need to equip a future habitat with seismic mitigation measures. The findings in this work suggest that due to epistemic uncertainty, significant disturbances may result in long-lasting vibrations that need to be considered as they may require effective mitigation measures.

## INTRODUCTION
In recent years, global interest in Lunar exploration has surged, mirroring the enthusiasm of the Apollo era. While Lunar surface missions flourished from the 1950s to the 1970s, the following decades witnessed a hiatus in Lunar exploration. However, the landscape has shifted, with NASA and private entities now heavily investing in returning to the Moon, making it a short-term goal within their broader mission to reach Mars. Under NASA's Artemis program, plans are underway for a short-term surface mission by the end of this decade.

To support these ambitions, it is crucial to explore surface habitat concepts for Lunar missions, with a particular focus on inflatable habitats. Inflatable habitats offer an advantage by significantly increasing payload volume upon deployment on the Lunar surface. The concept of space habitation in inflatable structures has been in existence for decades, but its practical application was first tested with the Bigelow Expandable Activity Module (BEAM) module on the ISS. The BEAM module occupied 3.6 m³ on the launch vehicle and expanded to an operational volume of 16 m³ (Valle et al. 2019; Valle and Wells 2018). Currently, Sierra Space is in the process of developing their Large Integrated Flexible Environment (LIFE) orbital habitat, positioned to serve as the next-generation space station after the retirement of the ISS, boasting a claimed operational volume of 270 m³ (Morgan et al. n.d.). NASA, in its Moon-to-Mars Architecture definition document and various other NASA sources, has used the example of a surface habitat consisting of an inflatable torus on top of a lander (NASA 2023b).

Despite these developments, the impact of seismic motions on Lunar habitats has not received attention. Lunar seismic research, stemming from Apollo missions, has uncovered that the Moon experiences seismic activity, being the most critical events for Lunar infrastructure the shallow moonquakes. Dr. Ceri Nunn from NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California, the lead scientist in the project SeisMo recently re-analyzed the data from the five seismometers left behind by the Apollo astronauts. Based on the observations from the study Dr. Nunn indicated that possibly the upper layer of the Moon's surface is likely highly fractured, and up to 100 km thick, both of which disturb the movement of seismic waves across the surface. Currently there are no active seismometers on the Moon. The limited data hinders precise vulnerability assessment for Lunar habitats in the face of these shallow moonquakes, and its importance has been a low priority due to the claim that the habitat and its content are designed to survive the rigors of the launch environment vibrations. From the engineering perspective, Wootton and Jablonski 2021, Mottaghi and Benaroya 2015, have concluded on the need to evaluate how moonquakes can affect future Lunar infrastructure. This raises a critical question, given that inflatable Lunar habitat designed for short-to-long-term missions is engineered for launch vibration conditions, will shallow moonquakes pose a significant risk to its structural integrity? If so, how should this vulnerability be assessed to determine the potential impact on the habitat's contents?

This paper aims to address these concerns, comparing the performance of Class I Lunar habitats under launch vibrations and shallow moonquakes. We present a case study, analyzing habitat performance under these two scenarios and outlining an approach for assessing vulnerability concerning the mission duration. The results offer valuable insights for Lunar surface missions.

## CASE STUDY
In this study, the Class I habitat chosen for analysis comprises a torus-shaped inflatable structure mounted atop a lander housing the environmental control and life support system (ECLSS) universal pallet design from Collins Aerospace (O'Neill et al. 2019). The torus stands at a height of 6 meters and encompasses two floors, supported by a cantilever hydraulic jack system. The core of the torus consists of solid aluminum vertical members, leaving open spaces for equipment installation. The connection between the torus and the lander is assumed to be facilitated by an aluminum pedestal. For reference, the lander is assumed to have dimensions similar to those of the descent stage of the Lunar module (LM). Notably, the Lunar habitat exhibits distinct dynamic configurations for both launch conditions and when fully deployed on the Lunar surface. Within the habitat, the ECLSS universal pallet studied is situated on the second floor.

The ECLSS universal pallet is composed of a frame featuring four aluminum elements, incorporating lateral restraints both at the supports and mid-height points. The dimensions of the pallet are assumed as 0.60mx0.60mx1.80m [25”x25”x70”] according to the suggestions from (O'Neill et al. 2019). It is assumed that the pallet will be supported at both its top and bottom via pin connections. The universal pallet is assumed to be positioned within the aluminum core of the torus, as this space remains unchanged between deployment conditions.

A time-stepping technique with modal simplification using Newmark's method is employed to solve the fundamental dynamic equation of motion. The damping matrix is constructed with the assumption of classical damping at 5% of critical damping. Consequently, it is important to thoroughly examine the dynamic loads imposed by the launch environment on the universal pallet. This step is crucial for establishing a reference point for assessing the integrity of the state-of-the-art structural design of the habitat towards the seismic motions that may experience on the Lunar surface after deployment.

## LAUNCH DYNAMIC ENVIRONMENT
In the context of rocket launches, payloads face a range of dynamic loads, including those stemming from engine ignition, liftoff release, aerodynamic pressures, and maneuvering forces. These loads originate from various sources, such as acoustic and vibration excitations, and must be carefully assessed to ensure the structural integrity and functionality of the spacecraft and its payloads. All of these loads are encapsulated in a set of test requirements that hinge on the specific characteristics of the payload (NASA, 2023). Among other required vibration tests, the sine sweep test and the random vibration test are most common.

Commercial spacecraft such as the Atlas V and Falcon vehicles provide initial recommendations for the sine vibration test, which can later be refined through the use of couple load analysis (CLA), and offer guidelines for high-frequency random vibration tests (SpaceX, 2021; United Launch Alliance, 2010). To generate the sine sweep time history, specifications for lateral loads from both companies are adhered to, with a duration matching the rate of 4 octaves per minute (NASA, 2017). Similarly, the synthesizing of a time history in accordance with the power spectral density (PSD) outlined in NASA's standard minimum workmanship criteria and Falcon vehicle recommendations is used, with a set duration of 2 minutes, following NASA's stipulated requirements (NASA, 2017).

## LUNAR SEISMIC MOTION ENVIRONMENT
The seismic data collected by the Apollo Passive Seismic Experiment (APSE) was initially recorded on analog tape. To ensure accuracy and reliability, we employed the raw seismic data from (Nunn et al., 2022), which was formatted in the Standard for the Exchange of Earthquake Data (SEED). Furthermore, we adopted the data processing and refinement methodology outlined in their work for the purpose of cleaning and analyzing the seismic data. Among the extensive seismic dataset comprising approximately 12,000 events (Nakamura et al. 1982), the seismic motions of particular interest are the 28 shallow moonquakes, also known as High-Frequency Teleseismic events (HFT). These events represent the most energetic seismic occurrences observed during the 8-year campaign.

## ASSESSMENT OF LUNAR SEISMIC VULNERABILITY
The engineering seismic risk analysis requires the hazard characterization and the identification of vulnerabilities. A widely used and accepted method to characterize the hazard is detailed in (Cornell, 1968). This approach has undergone significant evolution over the decades, with contributions from various authors culminating in what is now known as Probabilistic Seismic Hazard Analysis (PSHA). To this day, PSHA relies on two main inputs: Ground Motion Prediction Equations (GMPEs) and site-specific magnitude-recurrence data. 

Considering the current infancy of applying PSHA on the Moon, a process to assess the vulnerability of a class I inflatable Lunar habitat is presented. The proposed process for assessment of seismic vulnerability is aimed at structural engineers with the goal of establishing the impact of a strong motion would represent for critical non-structural habitat contents. 

## NUMERICAL SIMULATION
Implementing the procedure on the case study, as defined in the previous section, and considering all aspects of the analysis, reveals that seismic motion produces distinct responses in terms of amplitude, frequency content, and duration for the Class I Lunar habitat. Additionally, the seismic motion introduce a risk into the habitat's design associated with the mission duration due to the levels of uncertainty inherent in the engineering process.

First, the response of the primary structure is evaluated using a recorded event from the Lunar seismic environment, with a scaled value of 1.0gL arbitrarily selected as the maximum expected peak ground acceleration for the habitat. The deployed structure exhibits a maximum displacement of 2.4cm, while the undeployed structure, within the payload fairing, experienced an estimated maximum displacement of 0.6cm. Furthermore, the displacement response time histories reveal that this response due to the Lunar seismic environment lasts for over an hour, whereas the launch environment's effect only persists for a few minutes.

Following an iterative process, a scale factor of 3.0gL is identified as the ground acceleration that closely approximated the deformation responses of the structures. With the responses obtained for the primary structure under consideration of both scale parameters, 1.0gL and 3.0gL, the accelerations at the supports of the non-structural elements were transferred as ground accelerations for the latter. Subsequently, the responses of the ECLSS under both scaling parameters are presented. In the case of 1.0gL, the deformed shape indicated a maximum displacement of 0.6cm for the deployed state and 0.1cm for the ECLSS inside the undeployed habitat. Transitioning to the 3.0gL case, the response shape remained similar, with displacements of approximately 0.6cm for both scenarios.

## DISCUSSION
The dynamic conditions during launch and within the Lunar surface habitat exhibit significant variations in terms of both amplitude and frequency content of dynamic forces. Employing the proposed approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of the potential impact of shallow seismic motions on the habitat's contents concerning different mission durations.

The Lunar seismic environment primarily operates at low frequencies compared to the launch dynamic environment, less than 10Hz, which can resonate with certain Lunar surface habitat designs, similar to the case study utilized in this work. Additionally, the prolonged duration of Lunar seismic events could have further implications for material integrity, particularly in relation to fracture and fatigue.

It is evident that, depending on the dynamics of the primary structure and the non-structural elements, they may resonate with the frequency content of the lunar seismic environment. These frequencies are not accounted for in the launch vibration design. As depicted in the case study, the primary structure exhibited a greater transverse response for 1.0gL than anticipated during launch. However, the ECLSS, with a higher fundamental frequency, had greater lateral response from the launch-induced vibrations. This disparity shows how susceptible the response is to both the primary structure and non-structural elements design. Therefore, there is a need for future surface settlements, especially for long-term use, to consider the effects of the Lunar seismic environment, as more seismic data is collected. 

A primary design trade made for space structures is to function effectively for mission objectives while optimizing material usage. This design philosophy conflicts with seismic design principles, which aim to accommodate the maximum expected motion. This approach is fundamentally problematic in a seismic context, where the optimization of a non-deterministic force inherently involves a probabilistic dimension.
