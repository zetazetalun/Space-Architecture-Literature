# Soils as extended composite phenotypes\n\n## Abstract\nSeveral recent theories and conceptual frameworks in pedology, ecology, geomorphology, and evolutionary biology suggest that earth's soils represent selective pressures and are expressions of the effects of genes through the effects of organisms (i.e., extended phenotypes). The cumulative, interacting, overlapping effects of these extended phenotypes as manifested in the soil represent an extended composite phenotype. This notion suggests that significant biological changes should be reflected in significant qualitative pedological changes. This is demonstrated via research in the Ouachita Mountains, Arkansas, where changes in selective pressures on trees linked to fire frequency are linked to changes in soil morphology. The concept has implications for the search for extraterrestrial life and understanding Earth as a set of tightly-coupled systems.\n\n## 1. Introduction\nThis paper argues that Earth's soils are an expression of the cumulative impacts of the biosphere on surface processes. If soils are expressions of biological evolution, implications are profound for understanding the evolution of earth systems. Soils can be viewed as: biomantles (Johnson, 1990); extended phenotypes (Dawkins, 1982); and products of ecosystem engineers and niche construction (Odling-Smee et al., 2003).\n\n## 2. Conceptual Framework\n### 2.1. Soils as Biotic Constructs\nVan Breemen (1993) argued that soils are influenced by biota to increase net primary productivity. Mechanisms include weathering, increasing nutrient supply, and reducing nutrient losses.\n\n### 2.2. Biomantles\nSoils are increasingly viewed as continuously-mixed biomantles. For example, in Antarctica, penguins initiate the development of ornithogenic soils.\n\n### 2.3. Extended Phenotypes and Niche Construction\nEcosystem engineers alter the environment to influence selective pressures. Biogenic structures are built to maintain optimal growth conditions.\n\n## 4. Tree-soil Interactions\n### 4.2. Vegetation History\nThe forests of the Ouachita Mountains, USA, offer a test case. Fire frequency has been manipulated to change vegetation from hardwood-dominated forests to pine-dominated woodlands. This shift is reflected in soil morphology.\n\n### 4.4.1. Effects on Soil Morphology\n(1) Pines are the first trees to colonize rock surfaces, initiating weathering.\n(2) Pines are more likely to uproot, leading to biomechanical mixing of soils.\n(3) Soil mounding occurs beneath shortleaf pines, influencing rock fragment displacement.\n\n## 5. Discussion and Conclusions\nThe model of ecosystem engineers supports a view of soils as integrated ecosystems structured by keystone species. Soils as extended composite phenotypes imply a relationship between the bio- and pedospheres. The framework suggests that while an extinct biosphere (e.g., on Mars) might not leave atmospheric signals, its impacts on soil morphology would be more long-lasting, providing clues for extraterrestrial life.
