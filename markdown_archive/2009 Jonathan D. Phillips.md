# Soils as Extended Composite Phenotypes

**Author:** Jonathan D. Phillips  
**Journal:** Geoderma 149 (2009) 143–151  
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.geoderma.2008.11.028

## Abstract
Several recent theories and conceptual frameworks in pedology, ecology, geomorphology, and evolutionary biology, taken together, suggest the notion that earth’s soils are not just strongly influenced by biota, but represent selective pressures. These ideas point in the direction of many aspects of soils as expressions of the effects of genes through the effects of organisms (i.e., extended phenotypes). The cumulative, interacting, overlapping effects of these extended phenotypes as manifested in the soil represent an extended composite phenotype. If this is the case, then we should expect major changes in biological evolution to be reflected in major changes in the types of soils. The notion of genetic signatures in soil morphology also has implications for the search for extraterrestrial life.

## 1. Introduction
This paper argues that Earth’s soils are an expression of the cumulative impacts of the biosphere on surface processes. Soils are viewed not just as media for plant growth or engineering materials, but as "biotic constructs" and manifestations of biological evolution. 

### Conceptual Frameworks Included:
- **Biomantles:** Surficial layers continually mixed by floral and faunal turbation.
- **Extended Phenotype:** Effects of a gene on the environment (Dawkins, 1982).
- **Ecosystem Engineering & Niche Construction:** Organisms modifying their environment to influence selective pressures.
- **Biogeomorphology:** Tight coupling between biological, ecological, and geological processes.

## 2. Conceptual Framework
### 2.1. Soils as Biotic Constructs
Soils are influenced by biota to increase net primary productivity. Organisms affect soil texture, structure, erosion, organic matter, and chemistry. This weathering and physical transformation of rock/sediment makes the crust more favorable for growth.

### 2.2. Biomantles
Soils are increasingly viewed as continuously-mixed biomantles where biomechanical effects (bioturbation) are as significant as chemical ones. This leads to the formation of vertical texture contrast soils (TCS).

## 4. Tree–Soil Interactions (Case Study)
In the Ouachita Mountains, USA, research shows that biotic changes due to selective pressures (like fire frequency) are associated with significant changes in soil type and morphology. 

### Key Tree-Soil Contrasts:
| Feature | Shortleaf Pine (*P. echinata*) | Common Hardwoods |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Colonization | Early colonizer of rock surfaces | Later succession |
| Uprooting | More likely; creates mounding | Less likely; trunk breakage |
| Soil Mixing | Rapid biomechanical mixing | Slower mixing |
| Growth Rate | Rapid | Slow to Moderate |

## 5. Discussion and Conclusions
The notion of soils as extended composite phenotypes (ECP) suggests that soil morphology reflects the cumulative genetic effects of generations of organisms. This connects molecular scales (DNA) to the ecosystem level.

### Implications for Space Science:
- **Extraterrestrial Signatures:** An extinct biosphere (e.g., on Mars) might leave long-lasting impacts on soil morphology even if atmospheric signatures vanish.
- **Planetary Membranes:** Soils act as "planetary skins" that capture and transform energy, serving as a physical manifestation of a biosphere.
