# Soils as extended composite phenotypes

**Jonathan D. Phillips**

*Tobacco Road Research Team, Department of Geography, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY 40506-0027, United States*

## Abstract
Several recent theories and conceptual frameworks in pedology, ecology, geomorphology, and evolutionary biology, taken together, suggest the notion that earth's soils are not just strongly influenced by biota, but represent selective pressures. These ideas point in the direction of many aspects of soils as expressions of the effects of genes through the effects of organisms (i.e., extended phenotypes). The cumulative, interacting, overlapping effects of these extended phenotypes as manifested in the soil represent an extended composite phenotype. If this is the case, then we should expect major changes in biological evolution to be reflected in major changes in the types of soils. A brief review of paleopedology literature supports the notion of coevolution of soils and biota, not just in the sense of both responding to the same environmental forcings, but also with respect to explicit pedological expressions of biological change. The extended composite phenotype notion also suggests that significant biological changes should be reflected in significant qualitative pedological changes (i.e., fundamental changes in soil morphology beyond quantitative changes in specific short-lived soil properties). This is demonstrated via recent research in the Ouachita Mountains, Arkansas, where changes in selective pressures on trees associated with fire frequency are linked to changes in soil morphology. The concept of soils as extended composite phenotypes has repercussions for pedology, evolutionary biology, and interpretations of soils in other earth and environmental sciences. The notion of genetic signatures in soil morphology also has implications for the search for extraterrestrial life, and extends the notion of Earth as a set of tightly-coupled, densely interwoven systems.

## 1. Introduction
This paper argues that Earth's soils are an expression of the cumulative impacts of the biosphere on surface processes. As the product of the combined, interacting influences of (at least) geology, climate, biota, topography, and time, soils may represent the single best indication of the nature, state, and condition of earth surface systems (Albrecht, 1957; Lovelock, 1993; Retallack, 1994, 2001; Huggett, 1998; Richter and Markewitz, 1995; Phillips, 1999).

There already exist a number of valid, useful, and complementary ways of viewing soils—as media for plant growth, habitats and ecosystems, weathered mantles, potentially mobile surface debris covers, membranes, reactors, and engineering materials, to name a few (see, e.g., Ollier and Pain, 1996; Schaetzl and Anderson, 2006).

## 2. Conceptual framework
### 2.1. Soils as biotic constructs
Van Breemen (1993) reviewed the effects of organisms on soil texture, structure, erosion, organic matter content, chemistry, and outlined biological processes which generally make Earth's crust more favorable for plant growth. The latter include weathering and other physical transformations of rock and sediment, increasing nutrient supply, reducing nutrient losses, increasing water holding capacity, decreasing compaction, and increasing aeration.

### 2.2. Biomantles
Soils are increasingly viewed by pedologists as continuously-mixed biomantles (Johnson, 1990; Paton et al., 1995). Even in Antarctica, a study found that penguins had the greatest influence on soil properties, initiating the development of ornithogenic soils (Cannone et al., 2008).

## 5. Discussion and conclusions
The ECP (Extended Composite Phenotype) framework also suggests initial possibilities with respect to searches for life on other planets. A living biosphere would likely have a signal in atmospheric chemistry, but an extinct biosphere (such as may have existed on Mars) would be unlikely to leave its fingerprints in atmospheric composition. Similarly, a biosphere's effects on soil chemistry are likely to be transient, whereas impacts on soil morphology would be more long-lasting. Thus an explicit view of soils as ECP may yield useful clues about potential extraterrestrial pedologic signatures of life.
