# Geotechnical Engineering Considerations for Extraterrestrial Infrastructure Construction

**Authors:** James K. Mitchell and W. David Carrier III  
**Published in:** The Art of Foundation Engineering Practice Congress 2010 (ASCE)  

## Abstract
As space exploration moves toward manned and unmanned landings on the Moon, Mars, and asteroids, detailed knowledge of extraterrestrial soils and rocks is required for the design of foundations, excavation, surface vehicles, and transportation systems. In-situ resource utilization (ISRU) will be vital due to the high costs of transporting materials from Earth. This paper summarizes current knowledge of lunar and martian soil properties and states their implications for geotechnical engineering.

## Lunar Soil Properties
Lunar soil (regolith) is formed primarily by meteorite impact and consists of rock fragments, glass, and agglutinates. It is categorized as broadly-graded silty fine sand to sandy silt (SM or ML).

## Martian Soil Properties
Martian regolith is more complex than lunar regolith due to a history involving water, volcanic activity, and aeolian processes. Windblown dust (average size 0.003 mm) is ubiquitous and magnetic.

## Geotechnical Design Considerations
- **Trafficability:** Generally manageable on the Moon, but Mars presents hazards like soft dust zones and rocky terrain.
- **Dust:** Creates health and mission risks (spacesuit damage, solar array covering, equipment overheating).
- **Foundations:** Lunar soil can support high foundation loads at shallow depths due to rapid densification with depth. Martian bearing capacity is more site-specific.
- **Excavation:** On the Moon, vertical walls can remain stable up to 2m deep. Excavated soil exhibits bulking when recompacted.

## ISRU and Construction
ISRU objectives include extracting oxygen, metals, and using soil as construction material. Successful implementation requires advanced knowledge of drilling, sampling, and soil mechanics to overcome the $100,000/kg transport cost from Earth.
