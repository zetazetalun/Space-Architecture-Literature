# 3D printed sulfur-regolith concrete performance evaluation for waterless extraterrestrial robotic construction

**Journal:** Automation in Construction 165 (2024) 105571  
**Authors:** Ilerioluwa Giwa, Mary Dempsey, Michael Fiske, Ali Kazemian  
**Published:** 18 June 2024

## Abstract
By leveraging the capabilities of construction 3D printing, building structures in harsh extraterrestrial environments is conceivable. Sulfur concrete is a waterless construction material that offers great potential to replace Portland cement concrete (PCC) in extraterrestrial construction. The shape stability of 3D printed Martian sulfur-regolith concrete (SRC) was found to benefit from a lower substrate layer temperature. However, this comes at the cost of flexural strength, resulting in up to 53% strength loss. The printed SRC specimens demonstrated a significantly faster strength development rate (gaining about 85% of the ultimate strength after only 12 h) compared to the printed PCC. The printed SRC specimens also outperformed the PCC specimens in vacuum conditions at higher temperatures. Furthermore, modifying the SRC materials with Dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) resulted in up to 44% strength increase and minimized the sublimation rate of the printed specimens in vacuum, especially at an elevated temperature.

## 1. Introduction
To promote successful exploration efforts and enable a long-term presence on the Moon and Mars, supporting infrastructure such as landing and launch pads, habitats, and protective shields are needed. Given the inhospitable conditions and limited human workforce, conventional construction methods cannot be adopted. Extrusion-based Construction 3D Printing (C3DP) holds significant potential for off-world construction due to its robotic and autonomous capabilities. To avoid the prohibitive cost of transporting materials from Earth, in-situ resources (ISRU) must be used.

## 3. Methodology
### 3.1. Materials and mixture proportions
SRC materials were prepared using elemental sulfur powder (99.9% purity) as the binder and Mars Global Simulant (MGS-1) as the fine aggregate. MGS-1 is a high-fidelity basaltic soil analog. A sulfur content of 33% (by weight) was selected. Modified SRC (MSRC) mixtures utilized 10% DCPD (% sulfur weight).

## 4. Results and Discussion
### 4.1. Fresh-state properties
The average wet density of SRC and PCC materials were 2.310 g/cm³ and 2.096 g/cm³, respectively. SRC flowability and slump increased with temperature (130°C to 150°C).

### 4.2. Performance of printed SRC specimens
- **Shape Stability:** Layer deformations occurred only at the shortest interlayer time gap (45 s). At 120 s and 180 s gaps, no visible deformation was observed.
- **Flexural Strength:** Specimens printed at 150°C showed up to 19% higher flexural strength compared to those at 130°C. Longer interlayer delays (120 s and 180 s) resulted in up to 23% lower flexural strength compared to 45 s gaps due to 'cold joints'.
- **Modification (DCPD):** MSRC showed up to 44% and 34% increases in compressive and flexural strength respectively compared to unmodified SRC.
- **Recyclability:** Printed SRC was successfully remelted and reprinted without significant changes in workability or strength, supporting cradle-to-cradle sustainability.

### 4.3. Vacuum Performance
SRC specimens at 25°C showed negligible weight loss in vacuum. At 55°C, weight loss increased but was significantly lower in MSRC (up to 85% reduction compared to SRC). PCC specimens suffered substantial strength loss (up to 30% at room temp, 78% at 55°C) due to water loss and outgassing.

## 5. Conclusions
- Martian SRC was successfully 3D printed, proving the feasibility of waterless off-world construction.
- SRC demonstrates rapid strength development, achieving 85% of ultimate strength in 12 h, compared to 19% for PCC in the same period.
- DCPD modification improves mechanical performance and reduces sublimation rates by an order of magnitude.
- SRC is highly recyclable through remelting, whereas PCC is not.
