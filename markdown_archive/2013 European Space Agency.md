# Building a lunar base with 3D printing

**Date:** January 31, 2013

![Lunar base made with 3D printing. Credit: ESA](lunar_base_render.jpg)

(Phys.org)—Setting up a lunar base could be made much simpler by using a 3D printer to build it from local materials. Industrial partners including renowned architects Foster + Partners have joined with ESA to test the feasibility of 3D printing using lunar soil.

"Terrestrial 3D printing technology has produced entire structures," said Laurent Pambaguian, heading the project for ESA. "Our industrial team investigated if it could similarly be employed to build a lunar habitat."

### Design Concept
Foster + Partners devised a weight-bearing 'catenary' dome design with a cellular structured wall to shield against micrometeoroids and space radiation, incorporating a pressurised inflatable to shelter astronauts. A hollow closed-cell structure – reminiscent of bird bones – provides a good combination of strength and weight.

The base's design was guided in turn by the properties of 3D-printed lunar soil, with a 1.5 tonne building block produced as a demonstration.

![Multi-dome lunar base being constructed. Credit: ESA](lunar_base_construction.jpg)

### Construction Process
The UK’s Monolite supplied the D-Shape printer for ESA's 3D-printed lunar base study, with a mobile printing array of nozzles on a 6 m frame to spray a binding solution onto a sand-like building material. 3D ‘printouts’ are built up layer by layer.

1.  **Preparation:** Simulated lunar material is mixed with magnesium oxide. This turns it into 'paper' to print with.
2.  **Printing:** A structural 'ink' (binding salt) is applied which converts the material into a stone-like solid.

"Our current printer builds at a rate of around 2 m per hour, while our next-generation design should attain 3.5 m per hour, completing an entire building in a week," explained Monolite founder Enrico Dini.

### Challenges and Testing
Italian space research firm Alta SpA worked with Pisa-based engineering university Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna on adapting 3D printing techniques to a Moon mission and ensuring process quality control. 

*   **Vacuum Conditions:** Liquids typically boil away in a vacuum. To solve this, the 3D printer nozzle was inserted beneath the regolith layer. Small 2 mm-scale droplets stayed trapped by capillary forces in the soil, proving the process works in a vacuum.
*   **Simulated Materials:** The team identified a basaltic rock from a volcano in central Italy that has a 99.8% resemblance to lunar soil.
*   **Environmental Factors:** Further study is required for controlling lunar dust and managing extreme temperature fluctuations, though the lunar poles offer the most moderate ranges.
