# A microbial role in the construction of Mono Lake carbonate chimneys?

**Authors:** Alexander Brasier, David Wacey, Mike Rogerson, Paul Guagliardo, Martin Saunders, Siri Kellner, Ramon Mercedes-Martin, Tim Prior, Colin Taylor, Anna Matthews, John Reijmer
**Journal:** Geobiology, 2018; 16:540–555
**DOI:** 10.1111/gbi.12292

## Abstract
Lacustrine carbonate chimneys are striking, metre-scale constructions. If these were bioinfluenced constructions, they could be priority targets in the search for early and extraterrestrial microbial life. However, there are questions over whether such chimneys are built on a geobiological framework or are solely abiotic geomorphological features produced by mixing of lake and spring waters. Here, we use correlative microscopy to show that microbes were living around Pleistocene Mono Lake carbonate chimneys during their growth. A plausible interpretation is that benthic cyanobacteria and their associated extracellular organic material (EOM) formed tubular biofilms around rising sublacustrine spring vent waters, binding calcium ions and trapping and binding detrital silicate sediment. Decay of these biofilms would locally have increased calcium and carbonate ion activity, inducing calcite precipitation on and around the biofilms. Early manganese carbonate mineralisation was directly associated with cell walls. It seems likely that the biofilms provided initial sites for calcite nucleation and encouraged the primary organised crystal growth. Carbonate chimneys represent a promising macroscale target in the exploration for ancient or extraterrestrial life.

## 1. Introduction
Distinguishing biologically influenced sedimentary rock structures from abiotic ones is a key challenge in the search for extraterrestrial life. Free-standing chimneys composed of carbonate, sulphate, or silicate minerals provide a potential set of targets for terrestrial geological and astrobiological investigation. Mono Lake, California, is a globally important site for studying potential biogeochemical processes creating 'tufa' limestone chimney constructions around sublacustrine vents.

## 2. Methods
- **Fieldwork:** Conducted around Mono Lake (October 2014) focusing on Pleistocene chimney structures.
- **XRD Analysis:** Collected from ground samples using a PANAlytical Empyrean X-ray diffractometer.
- **Biomarker Analysis:** Soxhlet extraction and analysis on an Agilent 6890GC with 5975MS.
- **Microscopy:** Used Focused Ion Beam (FIB) preparation for TEM, NanoSIMS ion mapping, and SEM-EDS.

## 3. Results
### 3.1 Petrography of Chimney Pipes
The Mono City chimneys vary from 3 to 4 m in height and 1.5 to 3 m in width. Internally, they are constructed of stacks of numerous calcitic cones or pipes, each 30–60 cm in height and around 3 cm in width. Each pipe has a central 1 cm-sized void or conduit.

### 3.2 Microfossils
Clusters of filaments are ubiquitous within the columnar growths. 
- **Type 1 Filaments:** Diameters of 0.8 to 8 μm (peaks at 1 μm and 2.5 μm); likely empty sheaths.
- **Type 2 Filaments:** Diameters of 10 to 14.5 μm; well-preserved trichomes with interior mineralisation.
- **Coccoids:** 6.8 to 15 μm diameter, but rarer.

### 3.3 Chemistry
- **XRD:** ~99% of chimney pipe rock is calcite with an average Magnesium content of 8.1%.
- **Biomarkers:** Predominance of n-C15 to C18 n-alkanes with a maximum at C17, consistent with photosynthetic cyanobacteria.
- **NanoSIMS/TEM:** Show silicate nanograins (chlorite, feldspar, quartz) trapped by EOM surrounding the filaments.

## 4. Discussion
The thinolite (ikaite pseudomorphs) on the exterior likely represents abiotic environmental changes (temperature drop). However, the internal pipe fabric is microbially mediated. The EOM from cyanobacteria likely stripped calcium from rising vent waters, favoring calcite nucleation. The location and pipe-like morphology of the chimneys appear to be a result of biofilm growth around rising spring waters.

## 5. Conclusions
The Mono City chimney pipes are packed with fossil microbial filaments. These microbes influenced tufa chimney fabric development at the nano- and microscales. This has direct applicability to the search for extraterrestrial microbial life and understanding bio-mediated construction in extreme environments.
