# A microbial role in the construction of Mono Lake carbonate chimneys?

## Abstract
Lacustrine carbonate chimneys are striking, metre-scale constructions. If these were bioinfluenced constructions, they could be priority targets in the search for early and extraterrestrial microbial life. However, there are questions over whether such chimneys are built on a geobiological framework or are solely abiotic geomorphological features produced by mixing of lake and spring waters. Here, we use correlative microscopy to show that microbes were living around Pleistocene Mono Lake carbonate chimneys during their growth. A plausible interpretation is that benthic cyanobacteria and their associated extracellular organic material (EOM) formed tubular biofilms around rising sublacustrine spring vent waters, binding calcium ions and trapping and binding detrital silicate sediment. Decay of these biofilms would locally have increased calcium and carbonate ion activity, inducing calcite precipitation on and around the biofilms. Such carbonate chimneys represent a promising macro-scale target in the exploration for ancient or extraterrestrial life.

## 1. Introduction
Distinguishing biologically influenced sedimentary rock structures from abiotic ones is a key challenge in the search for extraterrestrial life. Free-standing chimneys composed of carbonate, sulphate or silicate minerals, if requiring the influence of organisms to develop, provide a potential set of targets for terrestrial geological and astrobiological investigation.

## 2. Methods
### 2.1 Fieldwork and sample processing
Fieldwork around Mono Lake was undertaken in October 2014. Pleistocene chimney structures close to Mono City were the focus of this article. 

### 2.2 XRD analysis
Three subsamples were crushed for XRD analysis to determine bulk rock mineralogy.

### 2.3 Biomarker analysis
Analysis was conducted using GC-MS to identify lipid biomarkers entombed within the rock.

### 2.4 Focussed ion beam (FIB) and TEM analysis
Dual-beam FIB system (FEI Helios NanoLab G3 CX) and TEM/STEM (FEI Titan G2 80-200) were used for high-resolution imaging and chemical mapping at the nanoscale.

## 3. Results
- **Chimney Dimensions:** The Mono City chimneys vary from c. 3 to 4 m in height and from c. 1.5 to 3 m in width.
- **Internal Structure:** Chimneys are constructed of stacks of numerous calcitic cones or pipes (30–60 cm high, ~3 cm width) with a central 1 cm-sized void.
- **Mineralogy:** XRD data showed ~99% of chimney pipe rock to be calcite with an average magnesium content of 8.1(1)%. Quartz constitutes ~1%.
- **Microfossils:** Clusters of filaments (Type 1: 0.8-8 μm diameter; Type 2: 10-14.5 μm diameter) are ubiquitous within the calcite growths.
- **Chemistry:** NanoSIMS and TEM-EDS show silicate nanograins (quartz, feldspar, chlorite) trapped in organic-rich areas (EOM) surrounding filaments.

## 4. Discussion
### 4.5 Biological influences on chimney growth
The majority of the calcite developed outside the microbial filaments. EOM initially inhibited crystal formation, but upon degradation, released calcium, making tubular sheets of EOM preferential sites for calcite nucleation. Even if crystal growth operated via abiotic processes later, the initial location and fabric control was bio-influenced.

## 5. Conclusions
The Mono City chimney pipes are packed with fossil microbial filaments. Microbes influenced tufa chimney fabric development at the nano- and micro-scales. These findings have direct applicability to the search for ancient and extraterrestrial microbial life and understanding natural structural formation in extreme environments.
