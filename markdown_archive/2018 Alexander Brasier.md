# A microbial role in the construction of Mono Lake carbonate chimneys?

**Alexander Brasier, David Wacey, Mike Rogerson, Paul Guagliardo, Martin Saunders, Siri Kellner, Ramon Mercedes-Martin, Tim Prior, Colin Taylor, Anna Matthews, John Reijmer**

**Published:** 2018 in *Geobiology*
**DOI:** 10.1111/gbi.12292

## Abstract
Lacustrine carbonate chimneys are striking, metre-scale constructions. If these were bioinfluenced constructions, they could be priority targets in the search for early and extraterrestrial microbial life. However, there are questions over whether such chimneys are built on a geobiological framework or are solely abiotic geomorphological features produced by mixing of lake and spring waters. Here, we use correlative microscopy to show that microbes were living around Pleistocene Mono Lake carbonate chimneys during their growth. A plausible interpretation is that benthic cyanobacteria and their associated extracellular organic material (EOM) formed tubular biofilms around rising sublacustrine spring vent waters, binding calcium ions and trapping and binding detrital silicate sediment. Decay of these biofilms would locally have increased calcium and carbonate ion activity, inducing calcite precipitation on and around the biofilms. Early manganese carbonate mineralisation was directly associated with cell walls. We suggest that the nano-, micro- and macroscale fabrics of these Pleistocene Mono Lake chimneys were affected by the presence of centimetre-thick tubular and vertically stacked calcifying microbial mats.

## Introduction
Distinguishing biologically influenced sedimentary rock structures from abiotic ones is a key challenge in the search for early and extraterrestrial life. Free-standing chimneys composed of carbonate, sulphate or silicate minerals, if requiring the influence of organisms to develop, provide potential targets for terrestrial geological and astrobiological investigation. Mono Lake, California, is a site for studying 'tufa' limestone chimney constructions around sublacustrine vents.

## Results
### Petrography of Chimney Pipes
The walls of the c. 3 cm wide chimney pipes are mostly 0.5 to 1 cm thick, constructed of columns of calcite that grew radially around the c. 1 cm wide central conduit. Calcite crystal microspar columns may branch and fan outwards resulting in millimetre to centimetre scale shrub-like morphologies.

### Microfossils within Chimney Pipes
Clusters of filaments are a major component of the chimneys. They are ubiquitous within the columnar to shrub-like calcite growths. 
- **Type 1 Filaments:** Diameters c. 0.8 to 8 xμm. Dominated by empty sheaths.
- **Type 2 Filaments:** Diameters c. 10 to 14.5 xμm. Comprise well-preserved trichomes with clear interior mineralisation.
- **Coccoids:** Diameters 6.8 to 15 xμm (rarer).

### Chemical Analysis
- **Mineralogy:** ~99% of chimney pipe rock is calcite with an average magnesium content of 8.1%. The remaining ~1% is quartz.
- **Biomarkers:** Hydrocarbon lipids show a predominance of n-C15 to C18 n-alkanes (max at C17), consistent with photosynthetic cyanobacteria.
- **Silicates:** Silicate nanograins (quartz, K-Na-rich aluminosilicate, chlorite) surround many filaments, spatially associated with preserved extracellular organic material (EOM).

## Conclusions
- **Biogenic Influence:** Chimney fabric development was influenced at nano- to microscales by microbes colonising vent sites.
- **Mechanism:** EOM stripped calcium from vent waters, favoring calcite nucleation and subsequent organized crystal growth. 
- **Application:** Fossil tufa chimneys represent a promising macroscale target in the exploration for ancient or extraterrestrial life and demonstrate bio-mediated mineral accretion.
