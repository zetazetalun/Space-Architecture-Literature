# Geotechnical Engineering Considerations for Extraterrestrial Infrastructure Construction

**James K. Mitchell**, Sc.D., P.E., Dist. M. ASCE
**W. David Carrier III**, Sc.D., P.E., Fellow, Life M. ASCE

## Abstract
It is only a matter of time until space exploration enters a new phase that will include additional manned and unmanned landings and explorations on the Moon, Mars, and perhaps on asteroids. Detailed knowledge of the soils and rocks will be needed for the design of foundations for structures, for excavation and underground space utilization, for design of surface vehicles and construction of transportation systems, for evaluation of their suitability as construction materials and development of construction methods. In-situ resource utilization (ISRU) can be expected to play a major role in these activities. Current knowledge about the rocks and soils on the Moon and Mars is summarized, with particular emphases on their compositions, geotechnical properties, and in-situ states.

## Geotechnical Properties

### Moon (Regolith)
Lunar soil consists of rock fragments, mineral grains, glass, and agglutinates. It is characterized as broadly-graded silty fine sand to sandy silt (SM or ML).

**Relative Density:**
- Surface: 0 to 30% (very loose)
- 5 cm depth: >65% (medium to dense)
- 30 cm depth: >90% (very dense)

**Compressibility Parameters (Table 2):**
- Compression Index (Cc) Loose: 0.3
- Compression Index (Cc) Dense: 0.05
- Recompression Index (Cr): 0.003

### Mars
Martian soil is more complex due to tectonic, volcanic, hydro, and aeolian processes. Current data suggests properties similar to moderately dense soils on Earth, such as clayey silt with embedded sands and granules.

## Engineering and Design Considerations
- **Trafficability:** Generally manageable on the Moon; Mars presents greater complexity due to rocky terrain and soft zones.
- **Foundations:** Lunar regolith reaches high density at shallow depths, capable of supporting high foundation loads. Martian bearing capacity is more site-specific.
- **Excavation:** Core tube and drill holes on the Moon remain open, suggesting stable vertical walls are possible to depths of 2m or more.
- **Dust:** Poses significant risks to health and mechanical systems on both bodies.

## In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU)
The objective is to extract elements/minerals from regolith and atmosphere to reduce transport costs ($100k/kg to the Moon). Lunar regolith and rock are identified as potential construction materials, necessitating geotechnical study for drilling, sampling, and tunneling.
