# Low-velocity impact response of lunar and Martian regolith simulants: Implications for lunar and Martian surface explorations

**Journal:** Physics of Fluids 36, 113318 (2024)  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0233884  
**Authors:** Takuma Ishii, Arata Kioka, Jyh-Jaan Steven Huang, Yoshiki Tabuchi, Yasuhiro Yamada  
**Published:** 5 November 2024

## Abstract
The granular mechanics of lunar and Martian regolith remain inadequately understood, impeding progress in successful exploration, landing, drilling, sampling, and construction activities on extraterrestrial surfaces. This study investigates the granular behavior of the lunar and Martian regolith under impact conditions. Impact cratering experiments were conducted for the lunar highlands (LHS-1), lunar mare (LMS-1), Martian regolith (MGS-1), and terrestrial silica sand (T-8). Findings demonstrate that crater depths of lunar highland and Martian simulants are about two times greater than those of terrestrial silica sand at impact velocities of 40–70 cm/s.

## I. Introduction
The regolith layer, composed of fragmental and unconsolidated rock material, covers the lunar and Martian surfaces. Understanding its geomechanical characteristics—strength, stiffness, and cohesion—is indispensable for designing successful landing systems and building infrastructure on these surfaces.

## III. Results and Discussion

### A. Crater depths
- **LHS-1:** 5.0 ± 0.7 mm (10 mm fall) to 8.3 ± 0.5 mm (25 mm fall).
- **LMS-1:** 3.0 ± 0.2 mm (10 mm fall) to 4.7 ± 0.3 mm (25 mm fall).
- **MGS-1:** 4.9 ± 0.9 mm (10 mm fall) to 7.7 ± 0.9 mm (25 mm fall).
- **T-8:** 2.0 ± 0.3 mm (10 mm fall) to 3.8 ± 0.1 mm (25 mm fall).

### B. Effect of physical and geotechnical properties
The difference in crater depth is attributed to porosity, cohesion, and internal friction. Notably, the irregular and angular shapes of regolith particles (unlike terrestrial sand) contribute to less resistance to impact, leading to deeper craters in lunar and Martian simulants.

## IV. Conclusions
The study highlights significant differences in granular behavior between Earth’s sand and extraterrestrial regolith. Lunar highland and Martian surfaces exhibit reduced resistance to impact due to higher porosity and specific particle morphology. These results have critical implications for foundation stability and the construction of landing pads or bases.
