# StarCrete: A starch-based biocomposite for off-world construction

**Authors:** Aled D. Roberts and Nigel S. Scrutton  
**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.1515/eng-2022-0390](https://doi.org/10.1515/eng-2022-0390)  
**Published:** 2023 (Open Engineering)

## Abstract
Robust and affordable technology capabilities are needed before a sustained human presence on the lunar and Martian surfaces can be established. A key challenge is the production of high-strength structural materials from *in situ* resources to provide spacious habitats with adequate radiation shielding. Here, we demonstrate the use of ordinary starch as a binder for simulated extraterrestrial regolith to produce a high-strength biocomposite material, termed StarCrete. Lunar and Martian StarCrete achieved compressive strengths of 91.7 and 72.0 MPa, respectively, which is well within the domain of high-strength concrete (>42 MPa).

## 1 Introduction
A sustained human presence on the lunar and Martian surfaces will require habitats with thick walls and ceilings for protection against radiation exposure and meteor strikes. Due to the high cost of transporting mass from Earth to space, bulk construction materials will be produced from locally available resources – a concept known as in situ resource utilisation (ISRU). Starch (amylum) is an abundant plant-based carbohydrate and the main source of calories in the human diet. The use of surplus starch as a binder for regolith would avoid the need for additional construction material fabrication equipment and supporting infrastructure.

## 2 Results and Discussion
Potato starch was found to be the most effective source, having an UCS of 17.7 MPa. Optimization using a statistical Design of Experiments (DoE) methodology was employed. For Martian regolith simulant (MGS-1), the highest compressive strength achieved in the experiment was 71.10 MPa.

## 3 Conclusions and outlook
In this work, we demonstrate that ordinary plant-derived starch can serve as an effective binder for extraterrestrial regolith. The advantages include risk reduction (edible binder), practicality, system integration (integration with food production), resourcefulness (low energy), and resource locality.
