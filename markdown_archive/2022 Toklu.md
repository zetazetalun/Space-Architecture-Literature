# Lunar soils, simulants and lunar construction materials: An overview

**Authors:** Yusuf Cengiz Toklu, Pinar Akpinar  
**Journal:** Advances in Space Research 70 (2022) 762–779  
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.asr.2022.05.017  
**Available online:** 20 May 2022

## Abstract
This paper presents an overview of using local materials in lunar construction. It covers the nature of in-situ soils from lunar missions, the variety of lunar soil simulants produced on Earth, and their application in producing lunar construction materials like cement, concrete, and brick.

## 1. Introduction
Civil engineering for extraterrestrial construction will rely on raw materials found on the Moon due to the high cost of transportation from Earth. The primary in-situ material is regolith, which can be processed into lunar cement, concrete, or bricks. Currently, fewer than 400 kg of original lunar samples are available for research, necessitating the development of terrestrial simulants.

## 2. Lunar missions and lunar soil samples
Historical missions include the Soviet Luna Program and the US Apollo Program. Recent additions include China's Chang’e 5 mission, which returned ~2 kg of regolith from a basaltic area in 2020. Lunar soils vary by location (mare vs. highlands), with differences in chemical composition (SiO2, TiO2, Al2O3, etc.) and particle characteristics.

## 3. Lunar soil simulants
A survey of 35 lunar projects shows that 13% of simulant needs are for construction and 17% for excavation. The paper lists 54 simulants developed up to August 2021, including MLS-1, JSC-1, FJS-1, CAS-1, and GRC-1. These simulants mimic the chemical and geotechnical properties of lunar regolith for terrestrial testing.

## 4. Lunar construction materials
### 4.1. Regolith-based ordinary cements and concretes
Research since the 1980s has explored using regolith as aggregate and potentially extracting CaO for cement. Complications on the Moon include the lack of liquid water and extreme temperature fluctuations causing evaporation or freezing of the mix.

### 4.2. Sulfur-based lunar concretes
Waterless concrete using sulfur as a binder is a major focus. Sulfur melts at ~119°C and hardens at ~148°C. It can reach 70-80% of its ultimate strength within 24 hours.

### 4.3. Polymer-based lunar concretes
Using binders like polyethylene or epoxy mixed with lunar regolith. Strength increases with polymer content, and some composites exhibit ductile behavior.

### 4.4. Geopolymerized regolith concretes
Formed through dehydration-polycondensation using alkali activators. High compressive strengths (~45 MPa) have been reported, though vacuum and heat can degrade performance.

### 4.5. Lunar construction materials with 3D printing
Additive Manufacturing (AM) is key for ISRU. Techniques include:
- **Binder Jetting:** Granular simulant mixed with liquid binder (e.g., DNA-1 simulant).
- **Sintering:** Solar, laser, or microwave energy used to bond particles.
- **Extrusion:** Printing liquid inks containing simulant and copolymers.

## 5. Conclusive remarks
Lunar construction using ISRU is still in its infancy. Future work must focus on systematic experimental studies to find the best materials for the harsh lunar environment, leveraging 3D printing and sintering technologies.
