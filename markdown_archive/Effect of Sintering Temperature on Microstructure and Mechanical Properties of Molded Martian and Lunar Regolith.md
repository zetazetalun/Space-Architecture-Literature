# Effect of Sintering Temperature on Microstructure and Mechanical Properties of Molded Martian and Lunar Regolith

**Authors:** Peter Warren, Nandhini Raju, Hossein Ebrahimi, Milos Krsmanovic, Seetha Raghavan, Jayanta Kapat, Ranajay Ghosh
*University of Central Florida, Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, Orlando, Florida, USA*

## Abstract
Cylindrical specimens of Martian and Lunar regolith simulants were molded using a salt water binder and sintered at various temperatures for comparing microstructure, mechanical properties and shrinkage. Material microstructure are reported using optical microscope and material testing is done using an MTS universal testing machine. The experimental protocol was executed twice, once using Mars global simulant (MGS-1), and once using Lunar mare simulant (LMS-1). The specimens were fabricated via an injection molding method, designed to replicate typical masonary units as well as the green stage of Binder Jet Technique, an important additive manufacturing (AM) technique. Results show that for both the Martian and Lunar regolith that the optimal sintering temperature was somewhere between 1100°C and 1200°C. The compressive strength for both the Martian and Lunar masonary samples, that received optimal sintering conditions, was determined to be sufficient for construction of extraterrestrial structures. The work demonstrates that both the Martian and Lunar regolith show potential to be used as extra terrestrial masonary and as parent material for extra terrestrial BJT additive manufacturing processes.

## 1. Introduction
The prohibitive cost of transporting raw materials from the Earth to either the Moon or Mars is the driving factor for pursuing in-situ resource utilization (ISRU). The lack of oxygen, extreme thermal cycles, solar and cosmic radiation are significant challenges when attempting to construct a base in these harsh environments... Regolith has also shown promise in its ability to withstand the thermal cycles and radiation in their respective environments. However, using regolith for construction remains a formidable challenge for ISRU.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Regolith Simulant
MGS-1 (Martian) and LMS-1 (Lunar) from Exolith labs were used for the experiment due to the insufficient or nonexistent quantities of actual regolith.

### 2.2. ISRU Binder
A pure distilled H2O mixed with a small amount of NaCl is used as the binder material (1/16 or 6.25% NaCl ratio).

### 2.3. Manufacturing method
Injection molding was used, utilizing 3D-printed PLA molds to replicate the green part composed of binder and powder similar to both binder jetting and injection molding.

### 2.5. Sintering Protocol
Samples were placed on alumina blocks and pre-sintered directly after molding at 200°C for 1 hour. Subsequent sintering was done at 1000°C, 1100°C, and 1200°C for 1 hour respectively with a heating ramp rate of 25°C per minute.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1. Microstructure Images
Images taken at 10X and 200X magnification demonstrate clear distinction between sintering at 1200°C and lower temperatures. The 1200°C sintered images display a glassy encasement of rocky particles, being smooth and glass-like to the touch.

### 3.2. Compression Testing
The 1000°C and 1100°C samples did not sinter together well enough to report significant compressive strength. The samples sintered at 1200°C were sturdy enough to report data on compression modulus and compressive strength.

### 3.3. Shrinkage Behavior
Negligible shrinkage (1%-2%) occurred for samples sintered at 1000°C and 1100°C. Substantial vertical and radial shrinkage occurred at 1200°C.

## 4. Conclusion
The study has shown both Martian and Lunar regolith have the potential to be implemented in an additive manufacturing method referred to as binder jetting. The most optimal sintering temperature is somewhere between 1100°C and 1200°C. The compressive strength of both samples was found to be in the range of brick, indicating the material's capability for the construction of load-bearing structures.
