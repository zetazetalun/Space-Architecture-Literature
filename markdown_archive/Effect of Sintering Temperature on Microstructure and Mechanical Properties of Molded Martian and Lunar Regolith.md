# Effect of Sintering Temperature on Microstructure and Mechanical Properties of Molded Martian and Lunar Regolith

## Abstract
Cylindrical specimens of Martian and Lunar regolith simulants were molded using a salt water binder and sintered at various temperatures for comparing microstructure, mechanical properties and shrinkage. Material microstructure are reported using optical microscope and material testing is done using an MTS universal testing machine. The experimental protocol was executed twice, once using Mars global simulant (MGS-1), and once using Lunar mare simulant (LMS-1). The specimens were fabricated via an injection molding method, designed to replicate typical masonry units as well as the green stage of Binder Jet Technique (BJT), an important additive manufacturing (AM) technique. Results show that for both the Martian and Lunar regolith that the optimal sintering temperature was somewhere between 1100°C and 1200°C. The compressive strength for both the Martian and Lunar masonry samples, that received optimal sintering conditions, was determined to be sufficient for construction of extraterrestrial structures. The work demonstrates that both the Martian and Lunar regolith show potential to be used as extraterrestrial masonry and as parent material for extraterrestrial BJT additive manufacturing processes.

## 1. Introduction
The prohibitive cost of transporting raw materials from Earth is the driving factor for pursuing in-situ resource utilization (ISRU). Martian and Lunar regolith are primary choices for parent construction materials due to their abundance and ability to withstand thermal cycles and radiation.

## 2. Materials and Methods
### 2.1. Regolith Simulant
Studies used MGS-1 (Martian Global Simulant) and LMS-1 (Lunar Mare Simulant) from Exolith labs.

### 2.2. ISRU Binder
A solution of pure distilled H2O mixed with 6.25% NaCl was used as the binder material. This mimics potential in-situ resources found in Martian salts and water deposits.

### 2.3. Manufacturing Method
Samples were created using an injection molding method into 3D-printed PLA molds to replicate the 'green state' of Binder Jet Technique (BJT) printing. The ratio was 3.5 parts regolith to 1 part binder.

### 2.5. Sintering Protocol
Samples were pre-sintered at 200°C for 1 hour, then sintered at high temperatures (1000°C, 1100°C, or 1200°C) for 1 hour with a heating ramp of 25°C per minute.

## 3. Results and Discussion
### 3.2. Compression Testing
Samples sintered at 1000°C and 1100°C were too brittle for significant testing. Samples sintered at 1200°C provided usable data.

**Table 1: Material properties of sintered Lunar and Martian samples.**
| Property | Martian Sample (1200°C) | Lunar Sample (1200°C) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Radial Shrinkage (%) | 10.54% | 6.17% |
| Height Shrinkage (%) | 16.03% | 18.13% |
| Compression Modulus (MPa) | 67.80 | 55.73 |
| Compressive Strength (MPa) | 25.46 | 21.73 |

### 3.3. Shrinkage Behavior
Significant shrinkage occurred between 1100°C and 1200°C. Shrinkage was anisotropic, with higher percentages in the vertical direction (up to 18.13% for Lunar regolith).

### 3.4. Mineral Composition
Sintering behavior is influenced by glass-rich basalt content (melting range 984°C–1260°C). Lunar samples contained 10% more glass-rich basalt than Martian samples, leading to higher deformation/shrinkage.

## 4. Conclusion
Both Martian and Lunar regolith can be implemented in BJT-based additive manufacturing using a simple salt water binder. Optimal sintering temperature is between 1100°C and 1200°C, resulting in masonry units with compressive strengths comparable to Earth bricks (21-25 MPa).
