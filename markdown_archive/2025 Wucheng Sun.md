# Additive Manufacturing of Lunar Regolith: A Review

**Authors:** Wucheng Sun, Fen Dang, Yan Zhou, Shifeng Wen, Cheng Zhou, Yusheng Shi, Lieyun Ding
**Journal:** Additive Manufacturing Frontiers 4 (2025) 200225
**Publisher:** Elsevier Ltd on behalf of Chinese Mechanical Engineering Society (CMES)

## Abstract
Lunar in-situ construction using additive manufacturing (AM) technology has emerged as a critical pathway for sustainable extraterrestrial exploration. This review systematically evaluates two dominant AM paradigms for lunar regolith processing: low-temperature deposition forming (material extrusion and binder jetting), and high-energy beam additive manufacturing (powder bed fusion and directed energy deposition). Low-temperature methods achieve moderate compressive strength with low energy consumption but face challenges such as binder dependency and vacuum instability. By contrast, high-energy beam techniques enable binder-free fabrication with better compatibility for in-situ resource utilization, though they suffer from porosity, high energy intensity, and geometric limitations.

## 1. Introduction
Space exploration and the establishment of permanent habitats on the Moon have become pivotal focuses for sustainable exploration. In-situ resource utilization (ISRU) aims to harness lunar materials to minimize dependence on Earth-bound logistics. Lunar regolith, a loose granular layer covering the surface, is the primary target for construction materials.

## 2. Low-Temperature Deposition Forming

### 2.1. Material extrusion (MEX)
Includes direct ink writing (DIW) and fused deposition modeling (FDM). 
- **Direct Ink Writing (DIW):** Focuses on biopolymer binding, matrix transformation, and alkali activation.
- **Biopolymer-bound soil composites (BSCs):** Achieved compressive strength of 39.7 MPa.
- **Alkali-activated geopolymers (AAGs):** Achieved 48.1 MPa compressive strength.
- **Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM):** Utilizes thermoplastic–regolith composites like ABS, nylon, and PEEK.

### 2.2. Binder jetting (BJT)
Selectively deposits liquid binders onto layered powders. The "d-shaped" method is a notable BJT technology for large-scale lunar construction.

## 3. High-Energy Beam Additive Manufacturing

### 3.1. Powder bed fusion (PBF)
- **Selective Laser Sintering/Melting (SLS/SLM):** Achieved compressive strengths of 4.2–31.4 MPa. Porosity remains high (40–49%).
- **Selective Solar Light Sintering (SSLS):** Uses solar concentrators to melt regolith. RegoLight project (DLR) achieved layered structures with <5 MPa compressive strength due to interlayer bonding issues.
- **Selective Microwave Sintering (SMWS):** Leverages microwave-absorbing properties for deep sintering (0.5 m depth).

### 3.2. Directed Energy Deposition (DED)
Involves simultaneous delivery of feedstock and energy. Demonstrated feasibility for dense, near-net-shaped structures with energy density of 2.12 J/mm².

## 4. Analysis and Discussion

| Category | Advantage | Challenges | Future prospects |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **MEX** | Versatile feedstock, low energy | Binder dependency, vacuum issues | Hybrid systems, vacuum-optimized extrusion |
| **BJT** | Scalability, complex geometries | Low strength, binder dependency | Binder chemistry optimization |
| **PBF** | High ISRU compatibility (no binders) | High porosity, slow process | Scalable solar concentrators, multi-energy systems |
| **DED** | High-density outputs, low waste | High energy demand, complexity | Portable energy systems, repair applications |

## 5. Conclusion and Prospects
Future research priorities include:
1. Multi-scale characterization of lunar regolith properties.
2. In-situ material formation and property regulation.
3. Integrated structure–material–process co-design.
4. Autonomous robotic construction systems.
5. High-fidelity lunar environment simulation.
