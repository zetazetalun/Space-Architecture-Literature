# Earthmoving for the extraterrestrial ## Abstract The United States spaceport, and more importantly, its technical landscape, operate in the background for the technological and political progress in pursuit of the extraterrestrial. Throughout the construction of the launch complexes on the Florida coastline, earthmoving became standard practice to elevate rocket pads above sea level and protect against rocket blasts. This article reveals a critical history of place-based science on Cape Canaveral through an evolution of earthmoving practices, from cultural commemoration to contemporary environmental crises. ## Introduction In 1950, the US Air Force selected Central Florida's eastern coastline for missile operations. Cape Canaveral offered military security and proximity to the equator for rocket efficiency. ## Historical Landscape Before rocket launches, indigenous groups like the Ais people created burial mounds up to 25 feet high. Later, the Canaveral Club (est. 1889) used dredging to allow boat transit, altering the local ecology. ## Spaceport Infrastructure Development The design of launch sites required specific earthmoving techniques. 70% of all launch complexes at Cape Canaveral were constructed between 1950 and 1961. Launch pads were elevated using hydraulic fill from the Banana River and pyramid-like sandpiles. The technology for the Missile Crawler Transporter was adapted from steam-shovel mining equipment used in Kentucky coal mines. ## Environmental Context and Future Risks In 1963, the Merritt Island National Wildlife Refuge was established, creating a 'techno-spatial' environment where militarized infrastructure protected natural areas. Today, rising sea levels and hurricanes (e.g., Hurricane Dorian) threaten these facilities, leading to new dredging and soil importation efforts.
