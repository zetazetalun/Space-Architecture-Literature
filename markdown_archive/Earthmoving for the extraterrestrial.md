# Earthmoving for the extraterrestrial

**Author:** Jeffrey S Nesbit (Temple University, USA)
**Published in:** EPD: Society and Space, 2025, Vol. 43(2) 245–261
**DOI:** 10.1177/02637758231204701

## Abstract
The United States spaceport, and its technical landscape, operate in the background for the technological and political progress in pursuit of the extraterrestrial. Throughout the construction of the launch complexes on the Florida coastline, earthmoving became standard practice to elevate rocket pads above sea level and protect against rocket blasts. This article reveals a critical history of place-based science on Cape Canaveral through an evolution of earthmoving practices, from cultural commemoration and extraterrestrial imagination to contemporary environmental crises.

## Introduction
In 1950, the United States Air Force selected the Central Florida eastern coastline for missile operations and rocket launch facilities. Cape Canaveral was chosen for military security, proximity to the equator for rocket efficiency, and safety. The spaceport's technical landscape is dominated by earthmoving practices that displace soil while erasing natural and cultural histories.

## Historical Landform Typologies
The article identifies three landform typologies relevant to the region's development:
1. **Indigenous Mounds:** Burial and shell midden mounds (e.g., Turtle Mound) created by the Ais and Timucua people.
2. **Launch Complex Infrastructure:** Artificially manufactured ground used to block water and support rocket pads.
3. **Dredging History:** The manipulation of water courses and wetland ecologies for logistics and maritime access.

## Technical Engineering and the Spaceport
### The Peenemunde Model
The design of earthmoving techniques for early rocket sites was influenced by German architect Albert Speer and pioneers like Wernher von Braun and Kurt Debus. The launch pad at Peenemunde, bounded by an 'embankment' of built-up earthwork and electrically networked systems, became the model for Cape Canaveral.

### Construction Speed
Launch complex infrastructure rapidly increased during the Cold War. An astonishing 70% of all launch complexes at Cape Canaveral were constructed over a brief seven-year span from 1950 to 1961. By 1958, 23 of the total 36 launch complexes were already in place.

### The Crawlerway
NASA engineered large subterranean water channels (culverts) for the 3-mile-long crawlerway. The technology for the crawler transporter was adopted from steam-shovel mining equipment used for coal extraction in Kentucky. Two large pyramid-like sandpiles were constructed to elevate the launch pads for the Saturn V rocket.

## Environmental Threats and Climate Change
In 2016, NASA advised that rising sea levels and hurricanes are the greatest threats to the landscape. Beach erosion and storm surges (e.g., Hurricane Dorian in 2019) put active launch facilities at risk. Current remedies include dredging, importing new soil, and raising beach/dune elevations.

## Conclusion
The evolution of launch site infrastructure is a way to decipher the evolution of territory. Earthmoving practices in the militarized launch complex leave a stratification of ground and a fragmented history, now threatened by the very planetary forces (oceans) they sought to manage.
