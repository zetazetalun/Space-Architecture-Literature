# Bio-Manufacturing of Engineered Living Materials for Martian Construction: Design of the Synthetic Community

**Authors:** Nisha Rokaya, Erin C. Carr, Richard A. Wilson, Congrui Jin  
**Publication:** ASME Journal of Manufacturing Science and Engineering, August 2025, Vol. 147 / 081008-1

## Abstract
This study demonstrates that architecture on Mars can be realized by designing a synthetic community, including diazotrophic cyanobacteria and filamentous fungi, which produce large amounts of biomaterials to bond Martian regolith particles into a consolidated body. Through 3D printing, a wide range of structures can be fabricated, such as buildings, houses, tables, and chairs. Within the synthetic community, diazotrophic cyanobacteria will (1) fix carbon dioxide and dinitrogen from the atmosphere and convert them into oxygen and organic nutrients to help the survival and growth of filamentous fungi and (2) increase the concentration of carbonate ions by photosynthetic activities. Filamentous fungi will (1) bind metal ions onto fungal cell walls and serve as nucleation sites for biomineral production and (2) enhance the growth of cyanobacteria by providing them with water, minerals, and carbon dioxide. In this study, such coculture systems have been created and displayed robust growth solely based on Martian regolith simulants, air, light, and an inorganic liquid medium without any additional carbon or nitrogen sources.

## 1. Introduction
The challenges of Martian colonization include the impracticality of transporting all building parts from Earth. The goal of this project is to create biomaterials that glue Martian regolith particles into structures using microorganisms. A two-component synthetic lichen system is created where cyanobacteria fix CO2 and N2 into nutrients for fungi, while fungi provide water and minerals back to the cyanobacteria. Both components secrete biopolymers that enhance adhesion among regolith particles.

## 2. Background and Motivation
Existing methods for regolith bonding (magnesium-based cement, sulfur concrete, geopolymers) require significant human assistance or the extraction of scarce materials. Microbe-mediated self-growing technology is a promising alternative, though existing single-species systems lack autonomy and require continuous nutrient supply. This project aims for a completely autonomous system using multiple species.

## 3. Design Considerations
Filamentous fungi are selected over bacteria due to their superior survivability in harsh conditions and better biomineral production. A synthetic lichen system pairs these fungi with diazotrophic cyanobacteria to create a self-supporting nutrient cycle. Alkaliphilic strains are prioritized to survive the high pH (approx. 9.5) of Martian regolith simulants like MGS-1.

## 4. Materials and Methods
- **Strains:** Alkaliphilic cyanobacteria and fungi collected from natural high-pH environments (e.g., Nottingham Serpentine Barrens).
- **Testing:** Survivability and mutualism testing using four steps (survivability, mutualism, quantitative characterization, and selection). 
- **Characterization:** Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS), and Benedict’s test for sugar detection.

## 5. Results
Six stable symbioses were identified consisting of *Anabaena sp.* and various fungi including *Penicillium sipitatus*, *Aspergillus niger*, *Trichoderma reesei*, and *Trichoderma viride*. The cocultures of *Anabaena sp.* with *A. niger* and *T. reesei* demonstrated the most robust growth. EDS analysis confirmed the precipitated crystals were primarily calcium carbonate.

## 6. Implementation and 3D Printing
The project is developing a regolith ink for 3D printing biostructures using Direct Ink Writing (DIW). The ink consists of: 
1. Cyanobacterial and fungal cells
2. Martian regolith
3. Alginate hydrogel for initial bonding and printability.

The process follows three steps: initial crosslinking of alginate using calcium ions from regolith, printing in a vapor-containing calcium ion environment, and permanent biomineralization by the microbes.

## 7. Conclusion
Synthetic symbioses can successfully grow on Martian regolith simulants using only air, light, and water. This self-growing technology offers a pathway for sustainable extraterrestrial construction. Future work will focus on the mechanical strength of the resulting materials and the optimization of the 3D printing process.
