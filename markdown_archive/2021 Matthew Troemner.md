# Design and Analysis of 3D-Printable Thin-Shell Dome Structures for Extraterrestrial Habitation

**Authors:** Matthew Troemner, Elham Ramyar, Benton Johnson, and Gianluca Cusatis
**Affiliations:** Northwestern Univ.; Skidmore, Owings & Merrill LLP
**Publication:** Earth and Space 2021, ASCE

## Abstract
Extreme environmental conditions and novel construction techniques will dictate the form of any extraterrestrial habitat on Mars. This paper presents the design and analysis of a dome-shaped Martian habitat developed for NASA’s 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge. The structure features a composite hemispheric-parabolic dome optimized for 3D-printing on an inflatable pressure vessel using 'Marscrete'—a Martian concrete made from local regolith and sulfur. The study evaluates structural performance under wind, gravity, and meteorite impact loads.

## Design Basis
The primary drivers were constructability, usability, and functionality. To minimize overhanging regions and the need for supports, a hemispheric-parabolic dome was selected. The internal hemispherical form matches an underlying inflatable pressure vessel, which serves as both atmospheric control and a construction mold. The exterior parabolic form ensures that most external loads result in compressive stresses, suitable for the quasi-brittle Marscrete material. Mass optimization resulted in shell thicknesses ranging from 21 to 108 cm.

## Construction Method
1. **Site Preparation:** Autonomous rovers clear debris and prepare a shallow foundation.
2. **Foundation Printing:** An articulated arm-type 3D-printer prints the foundation.
3. **Deployment:** A collapsible dome pressure vessel is inflated on the foundation.
4. **3D-Printing:** The pressure vessel acts as a support backing. A novel printing scheme utilizes a crawler robot deployed at the apex by an articulated arm to circumnavigate and print layers from bottom to top.
5. **Finishing:** An oculus is affixed at the peak. The pressure vessel is slightly deflated to allow for independent thermal expansion.

## Environment and Loading
- **Gravity:** 3.71 m/s² (38% of Earth).
- **Wind Load:** Calculated as 9.79 Pa (conservative estimate at 70 mph), increased to 60.0 Pa for analysis to account for dust density.
- **Sand Buildup:** Static pressure load of 2,780 Pa applied to one half of the structure (0.5m depth at 1,500 kg/m³ density).
- **Meteorite Impact:** Risk assessed via Lattice Discrete Particle Model (LDPM) simulations.

## Load Analysis
Finite element analysis showed that under all typical load combinations (ASCE-7), the structure remains in the elastic region with deformations less than 1.0 mm. 

## Meteorite Impact Performance
Simulations identified a critical load threshold:
- **Critical Impact:** 7,390 grams mass at 6.29 km/sec velocity (surface-normal).
- **Entry Mass:** Corresponding atmospheric entry mass of approximately 12,022 grams.
- **Risk:** Probability of a hazardous impact during a one-year mission is approximately 6.9 × 10⁻⁹.

## Proposed Layout
The 93-square-meter layout is inspired by 'tiny house' designs and NASA HI-SEAS. It includes:
- A central round common area under a transparent oculus.
- Public sector: Kitchen, workspaces, laboratory.
- Private sector: Bedrooms and lavatory.
- Two entries: One personnel airlock and one docking port for the Multi-Mission Space Exploration Vehicle (MMSEV).

## Conclusions
The hemispheric-parabolic dome design successfully meets the requirements for a one-year Martian mission. The construction scheme eliminates the need for complex printed supports by using an inflatable backing, and the structural shell provides high performance under Martian loads and meteorite risks.
