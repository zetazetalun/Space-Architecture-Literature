# Designing Extraterrestrial Habitats on Mars and the Moon: An Overview of Environmental Conditions and Design Considerations

**Author:** Bengi Bayar  
**Affiliation:** Middle East Technical University, Ankara, Turkey  
**Published in:** Proceedings of the 4th International Civil Engineering and Architecture Conference (2025)

## Abstract
In the past few decades, the interest in constructing habitats on other celestial bodies has increased. This study aims to provide an overview of the environmental conditions (radiation, atmosphere, gravity, temperature) specifically on the Moon and Mars. It analyzes design considerations including technical, logistical, and human factors, suggesting that successful habitats require collaborative multidisciplinary research.

## 1 Introduction
The development of reusable space technologies has democratized access to space. Extraterrestrial habitats hold promise for scientific research and resource extraction, but require comprehensive assessment for success and long-term sustainability.

## 2 Methodology
This study investigates the environmental parameters of the Moon and Mars (atmosphere, radiation, temperature, soil, gravity), analyzes the hazards they pose, and explores potential strategies for mitigation.

### 3.1 Atmosphere and Interaction with Space
The Moon has negligible atmospheric pressure, while Mars has a thin atmosphere (0.007 atm) primarily composed of CO2. Both environments present significant dust challenges.

### 3.2 Radiation Exposure
Space radiation is a primary health hazard. The lack of atmospheric and magnetic shielding leads to high cancer risks. Passive shielding (material selection) is currently the main countermeasure.

### 3.3 Surface and Underground Temperature
Lunar temperatures vary from ~95 K to ~397 K. Mars has an average surface temperature of ~240 K. Surface habitats require significant power for thermal regulation.

### 3.4 Lunar and Martian Soil
Regolith (grain sizes 40-800 µm) is hazardous due to abrasiveness and toxicity but is an abundant source for ISRU.

## 4 Design Considerations

### Table 2: Environmental conditions, hazards, and habitat responses
| Condition | Hazards | Response |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Atmosphere | Radiation, micrometeoroids, vacuum | Protection, pressurized interior spaces |
| Radiation | Electronics failure, crew safety | Active/passive shielding, redundancy |
| Temperature | Fatigue in structures, thermal comfort | Insulation, site/material selection |
| Soil | Abrasion, efficiency loss for panels | Material selection, self-cleaning surfaces |
| Gravity | Crew health hazard | Artificial gravity, exercise spaces, diet |

### 4.1 Architectural Considerations
Architects must design for psychological well-being, promoting social behavior while allowing privacy. For a crew of 6 for 6 months, a minimum habitable volume of 150 m³ is suggested, with specific allocations for berthing (42.36 m³), recreation (49.95 m³), etc.

### 4.2 Engineering Considerations
Key challenges include life support (closed-loop systems), power generation (solar/nuclear), and material transportation (favoring ISRU).

## 5 Conclusion
Habitat design is governed by environmental factors and sustainability. Future research must focus on local resources for power, air, and water. Collaborative efforts between engineers and architects are critical.
