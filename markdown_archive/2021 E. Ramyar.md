# Numerical Simulation of 3D Printing of Infrastructure Materials

**Authors:** E. Ramyar, Z. Xinwei, G. Cusatis
**Published in:** Earth and Space 2021 (ASCE)

## Abstract
3D printing is a disruptive force for terrestrial and extraterrestrial infrastructure development. This paper highlights the formulation of the Discrete Fresh Concrete Model (DFCM), a particle-based cementitious material model that simulates rheological and mechanical behavior during and after printing. The model uses visco-plastic constitutive equations based on stress-strain relationships to allow a seamless transition from fresh to hardened concrete states.

## Introduction
Concrete 3D printing requires adapting technologies to the rheological characteristics of concrete and developing new materials. Computational models are essential for the design and optimization of printed structures, particularly for autonomous remote operations in extraterrestrial environments like Mars.

## Discrete Fresh Concrete Model (DFCM)
DFCM adopts rigid composite spherical particles to simulate coarse aggregate surrounded by soft mortar (the "excess paste theory").
- **Mechanics:** Contact behavior is governed by a combination of parallel and series nonlinear springs and viscous dashpots.
- **Constitutive Equations:** Formulated in terms of stress-strain relationships (unlike conventional force-displacement models), facilitating the use of parameters identical to those used for hardened concrete.
- **Key Parameters:** Tensile strength, mortar normal modulus, shear yield stress, plastic viscosity, and mortar thickness.

## Experimental Validation
The model was validated against two standard tests:
1.  **Slump Test:** Successfully simulated Self-Consolidating Concrete (SCC) spread (60 cm diameter/25 cm slump) and printable concrete (minimal slump of a few centimeters).
2.  **ICAR Rheometer Test:** Matched experimental torque-speed data for both regular and printable concrete mixes.

## Application: Northwestern Martian Habitat
In collaboration with Skidmore, Owings & Merrill (SOM), Northwestern University designed a dome-shaped Martian habitat for NASA’s 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge. The DFCM was used to simulate the layer-by-layer printing process, accounting for:
- Nozzle-particle and inter-particle rheological interactions.
- Material discharge rates dependent on gravity and pump pressure.
- Autonomous construction monitoring and optimization.

## Conclusion
The DFCM accurately simulates the rheological behavior of cementitious materials for 3D printing. The model's successful replication of experimental ICAR and slump tests demonstrates its reliability for simulating construction processes on other planets.
