# A review on design and construction of the lunar launch/landing infrastructure

**Authors:** Xinyi Li, Yuyue Gao, Yan Zhou, Wenbin Han, Cheng Zhou
**Journal:** Advances in Space Research
**Publication Date:** Available online 6 July 2024

## Abstract
With advances in automated construction and deeper Moon exploration missions, the concept of 'building a house on the moon' is becoming increasingly feasible. The launch/landing (LL) infrastructure plays a vital role in ensuring the safety of landers and astronauts and should be prioritized for construction. Initially, this review explores the variety of designs used at existing LL sites, analyzing the logic and unique features of each design. It then outlines three main construction methods for landing pads, from material production to basic construction processes. The review also covers experiments related to LL pads, including material, component, and construction testing. Finally, current challenges and future directions for the development of lunar LL infrastructure are discussed, and 3D-printed biomimetic structures are highlighted as a particularly promising option.

## 1. Introduction
The safety of launch and landing for space landers is key to accomplishing lunar exploration and construction missions. Unlike on Earth, the Moon lacks established launch and landing facilities, presenting significant safety challenges. Plumes ejected by lander engines cause damage to the lunar surface, and loose regolith can easily form large craters, exacerbating the unevenness of the lunar surface. High-speed regolith particles pose a serious threat by eroding surrounding equipment. A well-constructed launch/landing site is essential to ensure safe operations and control dust dispersion.

## 2. Design of the lunar landing/launching infrastructure

### 2.1 Launch/landing area
The size of the landing site is determined by the range of lunar regolith particle travel and the landing accuracy of the lander. Proposed diameters range from 18.8 m for small pads to 100 m for larger missions (e.g., Artemis/Olympus project). A zonal design is suggested to accommodate thermal and flow impingement.

### 2.2 Launch/landing pad
Two main design approaches: 
1. **Paving:** Simplest and most cost-effective, using 3D-printed stones or interlocking tiles.
2. **3D Extrusion Printing:** Allows for complex geometric structures, including hollow pie-shaped structures to mitigate plume effects.

### 2.3 Blast walls and trenches
Blast walls intercept high-speed dust ejecta. Designs include the 'Sunflower Vault' (inward-deflecting) and 'Eyelashes' (dust collection) concepts from SEArch+.

## 3. In situ construction for lunar launch/landing infrastructure

### 3.1 3D printing based on concrete extrusion
Involves mixing lunar regolith with polymers, sulfur, or geopolymer binders. 
- **Polymer concrete:** Sieve regolith mixed with polyethylene or RTV.
- **Contour Crafting (CCC):** Developed by Khoshnevis et al., capable of continuous flow printing of complex structures.

### 3.2 Sintering, melting and assembly
- **LASSS (Large Area Surface Sintering System):** Uses rovers with heaters to stabilize the soil surface.
- **Selective Separation Sintering (SSS):** An additive manufacturing approach using base powder and separator powder.
- **Solar/Laser Sintering:** Utilizing Fresnel lenses or CO2 lasers to melt simulant into interlocking pavers.

## 4. Terrestrial testing

### 4.1 Building material testing
Research focuses on mechanical performance and durability. 
- **Sintered samples:** JSC-1A reached full sintering at 1100°C.
- **Strength:** Simulant melting using a CO2 laser resulted in compressive strengths averaging 93.94 MPa.

### 4.2 Structural component testing
- **Interlocking forms:** Horizontal-vertical interlocking forms outperformed non-interlocking designs in plate load tests.
- **Hot fire tests:** Tested various materials (RTV pavers, basalt pavers with grout) under rocket motor plumes.

## 5. Challenges and future directions
- **Multiphysics modeling:** Difficulties in simulating plume-particle interactions in vacuum.
- **Cracking:** Preventing shrinkage-induced cracks in large-scale 3D prints.
- **Bionic Design:** Utilizing structures inspired by nature (e.g., sunflower, eyelashes) for erosion resistance.
