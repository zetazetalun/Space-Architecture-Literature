# 3D printed sulfur-regolith concrete performance evaluation for waterless extraterrestrial robotic construction

**Authors:** Ilerioluwa Giwa, Mary Dempsey, Michael Fiske, Ali Kazemian
**Journal:** Automation in Construction 165 (2024) 105571
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autcon.2024.105571

## Abstract
By leveraging the capabilities of construction 3D printing, building structures in harsh extraterrestrial environments is conceivable. Sulfur concrete is a waterless construction material that offers great potential to replace Portland cement concrete (PCC) in extraterrestrial construction. The shape stability of 3D printed Martian sulfur-regolith concrete (SRC) was found to benefit from a lower substrate layer temperature. However, this comes at the cost of flexural strength, resulting in up to 53% strength loss. The printed SRC specimens demonstrated a significantly faster strength development rate (gaining about 85% of the ultimate strength after only 12 h) compared to the printed PCC. The printed SRC specimens also outperformed the PCC specimens in vacuum conditions at higher temperatures. Furthermore, modifying the SRC materials with Dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) resulted in up to 44% strength increase and minimized the sublimation rate of the printed specimens in vacuum, especially at an elevated temperature.

## 1. Introduction
NASA’s Artemis program aims to establish a sustained human presence on the Lunar surface, serving as a precursor to Mars missions. Supporting infrastructure like landing pads, habitats, and protective shields are needed. Given the inhospitable conditions and limited human workforce, conventional construction cannot be adopted. Extrusion-based Construction 3D Printing (C3DP) holds significant potential owing to its robotic and autonomous capabilities. To avoid prohibitive costs of shipping materials from Earth, in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) is essential. Sulfur concrete (SC) is a waterless alternative well-suited for planetary construction, as sulfur is present on both the Moon and Mars.

## 3. Methodology
### 3.1 Materials and Mixture Proportions
- **Binder:** Elemental sulfur powder (99.9% purity).
- **Aggregate:** Mars Global Simulant (MGS-1).
- **Sulfur Content:** 33% by weight.
- **Modifier:** 10% Dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) by sulfur weight for modified specimens (MSRC).
- **Control:** Type I/II Portland cement concrete (PCC) with limestone powder and HRWRA.

### 3.2 Printing Systems
Two custom extrusion-based systems were used:
1. A gantry-type system for high-temperature materials (SRC), featuring a close-loop heating control and insulated screw-type extruder.
2. A system for cementitious materials (PCC).
Both used rectangular nozzles (40 mm x 20 mm).

## 4. Results and Discussion
### 4.1 Fresh-state properties
- **Wet Density:** SRC (2.310 g/cm³) > PCC (2.096 g/cm³).
- **Workability:** Flowability of SRC increased with temperature (130°C to 150°C).
- **Rheology:** Addition of regolith increased plastic viscosity of molten sulfur by up to 2 orders of magnitude.

### 4.2 Performance of printed SRC
- **Shape Stability:** Layer deformations occurred only at the shortest interlayer time gap (45 s). Longer delays (120 s, 180 s) allowed cooling and solidification, ensuring stability.
- **Flexural Strength:** A tradeoff exists; while 45 s gaps caused deformation, they provided the highest flexural strength due to better fusion. Longer gaps led to "cold joints" and up to 23% strength loss.
- **DCPD Modification:** Led to a 44% increase in compressive strength and 34% increase in flexural strength for mold-cast specimens. Printed MSRC was 41% stronger than printed SRC.
- **Recyclability:** SRC specimens were successfully remelted and reprinted with no significant change in flexural strength, demonstrating cradle-to-cradle sustainability.

### 4.3 Comparison with PCC
- **Strength Development:** SRC reached 85% of ultimate strength in 12 hours. PCC reached only 19% of its 28-day strength in the same period.
- **Vacuum Performance:** PCC suffered substantial strength loss (up to 30% at room temp, 78% at 55°C) due to moisture loss. SRC showed minor declines (7-12%) primarily due to sulfur sublimation. DCPD reduced the SRC sublimation rate by 85%.

## 5. Conclusions
- Martian SRC was successfully 3D printed, showing feasibility for waterless off-world construction.
- Geometrical accuracy and strength are highly dependent on extrusion and substrate temperatures.
- SRC exhibits faster strength gain and better vacuum resilience than PCC.
- DCPD modification enhances mechanical performance and minimizes sublimation.
- The material is fully recyclable through remelting without loss of property.
