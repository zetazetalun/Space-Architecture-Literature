# A Framework for Extraterrestrial Environmental Assessment\n\n**Author:** William R. Kramer\n**Affiliation:** Outer Space Consulting, Frederick, MD, 21701, USA\n**Journal:** Space Policy 53 (2020) 101385\n\n## Abstract\nMany proposed activities on the Moon, Mars, and elsewhere will require construction and similar actions that alter natural environments. These impacts may foreclose future use, hinder resource access, or impede human occupation. This article proposes a simple framework for Extraterrestrial Environmental Assessment (EEA) to identify, minimize, and mitigate harm, suggesting that space industries should develop their own assessment processes rather than relying on terrestrial models like the US National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA).\n\n## 1. A Rapidly Evolving Problem in Space Development\nPhysical activities such as construction, mining, and human habitation on the Moon and Mars may adversely affect those environments. Alterations to alien landscapes could be permanent due to the lack of resilient forces found on Earth (water, vegetation, tectonic movement). Despite awareness of potential fragility, few space industries recognize the range of environmental impacts their actions beyond LEO will generate.\n\n## 2. The Neglected Need for Extraterrestrial Environmental Assessment\nCompetition for resources (e.g., 'first in time' ventures) creates economic incentives to target easily accessible resources, potentially wasting them. Mining activities could create dust that coats solar panels, vibrations that invalidate seismic data, or chemical leaks that contaminate subsurface ice. 'Exogeoconservation' is proposed as necessary for responsible stewardship.\n\n## 3. National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) and Potential Sources of Reticence\nWhile NEPA is successful in the US, it is considered the 'wrong tool' for extraterrestrial jobs because:\n- It requires a US federal nexus.\n- It is cumbersome, costly, and leads to lengthy litigation.\n- It lacks international authority.\n- Current US policy does not consider celestial bodies as global resources requiring NEPA-style oversight.\n\n## 4. Framework for EEA\nAn EEA should support the Outer Space Treaty by ensuring the wise use of the global commons. The proposed framework includes:\n\n### Proposed EEA Structure\n1. **Introduction**\n2. **Purpose and Need:** Why is the action being undertaken?\n3. **Proposed Action:** Scope, equipment, timing, and abandonment plans.\n4. **Affected Environment:**\n   - *Physical Resources:* Geology, regolith, seismicity, atmospheric composition, climate, solar radiation.\n   - *Water Resources:* Location, state, chemistry, and intended use/extraction volume.\n5. **Cultural Resources:** Indicators of previous human actions or historical significance.\n6. **Hazardous Materials and Pollutants:** Use and disposition of chemicals/waste.\n7. **Human Health and Safety:** Number of personnel and mission-specific risks (radiation, etc.).\n8. **Cumulative Impacts:** Long-term effects of successive mining or construction actions.\n9. **Summary:** Mitigation actions to reduce or eliminate adverse impacts.\n\n## 5. Final Thoughts\nEEAs should be digitally filed and made available for public reference, creating a permanent record of pre-action baseline conditions. A consortium of nongovernmental space actors could monitor the process and maintain an open library of assessments.\n\n## 6. Next Actions\nSpace industries should take the lead in developing standards that achieve environmental goals while remaining practical for commercial and scientific objectives.
