# Extraterrestrial Construction in Lunar and Martian Environments

**M. Z. Naser** and **Qiushi Chen**

**Abstract:** Space exploration and terraforming nearby planets have been fascinating concepts for the longest time. Nowadays, technological advancements in manufacturing, robotics, and propellants are thriving, it is only a matter of time before humans can start colonizing nearby moons and planets. In order to facilitate feasible and sustainable space exploration, such habitats are envisioned to be primarily built from lunar and Martian in-situ resources. This paper explores the suitability of construction materials derived from lunar and Martian regolith, along with terrestrial derivatives, for interplanetary construction. This paper also identifies key processing techniques suitable to produce extraterrestrial construction materials under alien environments (i.e., vacuum, low gravity, etc.) and showcases prominent design concepts for “space-resilient” habitats and colonies.

## Introduction
Whether we pursue lunar or Martian colonization first, space explorers are to be housed in habitats that not only need to withstand extreme environments, but also preferably fabricated from in-situ resources. Transporting material to the Moon can cost up to $10,000 per pound. Utilizing in-situ resources promotes the development of independent and sustainable space habitats, though characterization and processing under microgravity, radiation, and vacuum remains challenging.

## Lunar and Martian Environments
Lunar and Martian surfaces hold harsh environments including vacuum, space debris, high radiation, and rapid temperature fluctuations. The gravity on the Moon and Mars is approximately 17% and 38% of Earth's, respectively. The Moon experiences a hard vacuum (3×10⁻¹³ kPa), while the Martian atmosphere is approximately 0.7 kPa.

## Lunar and Martian In-Situ Resources
NASA analysis of lunar soil shows substantial amounts of alumina, calcium oxide, and silicate. Remote sensing of Mars confirms similar compounds.

## Extraterrestrial Construction Materials

### Concrete-like derivatives
Sulfur concrete, geopolymer concrete, polymer concrete, and waterless concrete have high potential for space habitats due to raw material abundance.

### Metals
Regolith can be processed to produce aluminum (Al), magnesium (Mg), iron (Fe), and titanium (Ti).

### Advanced materials
Composites, shape memory materials, and self-sensing/self-healing materials are under investigation, though they lack space-environment verification.

## Processing of Extraterrestrial Construction Materials

### Sintering
Heating powder below melting point using solar, microwave, or laser energy. Successes include 0.1 mm layers at 1000°C and microwave sintering at 2.45 GHz.

### Cold-pressing
Leverages adhesive forces within regolith particles (especially smectite-like clay) and water moisture to create solids.

### Biocementation
Microbially induced calcite precipitation (MICP). Photosynthetic microalgae (e.g., Haematococcus pluvialis) can use CO2 and urea to precipitate CaCO3 in regolith.

## Concepts for Habitats
1. **Rigid Habitats:** Designed to accommodate loads without secondary systems; high redundancy but high transportation volume.
2. **Inflatable Habitats:** High packing coefficient; shipped folded and inflated via automated process.
3. **Underground Habitats:** Utilization of lunar/Martian lava tubes (kilometers long) to provide natural shielding.
4. **Mobile Habitats:** Habitats formed by independent rovers to improve exploration range.

## Conclusions
Space habitats can be grouped into rigid, inflatable, underground, and mobile types. Key challenges include improving the efficiency of space-based processing and minimizing energy needs.
