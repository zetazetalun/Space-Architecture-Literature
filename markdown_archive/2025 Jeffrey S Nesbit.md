**Jeffrey S Nesbit**

## Abstract
The United States spaceport, and more importantly, its technical landscape, operate in the background for the technological and political progress in pursuit of the extraterrestrial. Throughout the construction of the launch complexes on the Florida coastline, earthmoving became standard practice to elevate rocket pads above sea level and protect against rocket blasts. However, a more extended history of earthmoving at Cape Canaveral is necessary. From Earth’s early geological formations and indigenous burial mounds to the modernization of rockets, Cape Canaveral presents itself as an evolution of terrestrial form. And still, earthmoving continues today. In 2016, an article advised the greatest threats to NASA’s landscape are rising sea levels and hurricanes, causing substantial erosion to the beach, leaving active and historically significant launch facilities at risk. Cape Canaveral beaches are now preparing for additional dredging, importing new soil, and raising beach and dune elevations. This article reveals a critical history of place-based science on Cape Canaveral through an evolution of earthmoving practices, from cultural commemoration, extraterrestrial imagination, and contemporary environmental crises.

## Introduction
In 1950, the United States Air Force selected the Central Florida eastern coastline to construct its missile operations and rocket launch facilities. Cape Canaveral proved to be a “suitable” decision for military security, proximity to the equator for rocket efficiency entering orbital trajectory, and safety as failed rockets could discharge in the ocean. The spaceport, and more importantly, its technical landscape dominated by earthmoving practices, operate in the background for the technological and political progress in pursuit of the extraterrestrial. 

## Historical Formation and Indigenous Earthworks
Long before rocket launches, indigenous groups like the Ais people inhabited Cape Canaveral. They created commemorative burial mounds, sculpting the land to mark the dead. Some mounds reached up to 25 feet high and 100 feet across. These early manipulations of the landscape represent the first episodes of earthmoving in the region.

## Spaceport Infrastructure as Technical Landscape
Under the name Cape Canaveral Air Force Missile Test Annex, the site rapidly increased its launch complex infrastructure in the 1950s. Architects and engineers designed a 'spaceport anatomy' including semi-buried concrete blockhouses, elevated launch pads, and rocket service towers. 70% of all launch complexes at Cape Canaveral were constructed over a brief seven-year span from 1950 to 1961.

### Construction Techniques
*   **Dredging**: Engineers used hydraulic fill from the Banana River to elevate rocket launch pads above shallow sea level.
*   **Sandpiles**: Two large pyramid-like sandpiles were constructed to elevate pads used for the Saturn V rocket.
*   **Mining Equipment**: The Missile Crawler Transporter Facility adopted technology from surface coal mining (steam-shovel crawlers).

## Environmental and Climate Challenges
Today, the landscape is threatened by rising sea levels and hurricanes. In 2016, NASA reported that erosion puts active launch facilities at risk. Current practices involve dredging, importing new soil, and raising beach/dune elevations to protect the technical infrastructure.

## Conclusion
The evolution of launch site infrastructure deciphered through earthmoving practices reveals how territory is captured for extraction and deployment. The militarized infrastructural launch complex leaves a stratification of ground and fragmented history, now facing new threats from climate change as the planetary ocean reclaims the land.
