# Low-velocity impact response of lunar and Martian regolith simulants: Implications for lunar and Martian surface explorations

**Cite as:** Phys. Fluids 36, 113318 (2024); **doi:** 10.1063/5.0233884
**Submitted:** 18 August 2024 • **Accepted:** 3 October 2024 • **Published Online:** 5 November 2024

**Authors:**
Takuma Ishii, Arata Kioka, Jyh-Jaan Steven Huang, Yoshiki Tabuchi, and Yasuhiro Yamada

## ABSTRACT
The granular mechanics of lunar and Martian regolith remain inadequately understood, impeding progress in successful exploration, landing, drilling, sampling, and construction activities on extraterrestrial surfaces. This study aims to bridge this knowledge gap by investigating the granular behavior of the lunar and Martian regolith under impact conditions. Impact cratering experiments were conducted for the lunar highlands, lunar mare, Martian regolith simulants (LHS-1, LMS-1, and MGS-1, respectively), and terrestrial silica sand with similar particle sizes as target granular materials, with a sphere projectile dropping at low velocities. Our findings demonstrate that the crater depths of regolith layers of the lunar highlands and Martian surfaces are greater than those of the lunar mare regolith and terrestrial silica sand layers. Additionally, our power-law scaling highlights less resistance to crater impact in the lunar and Martian regolith layers than in the terrestrial sand layer.

## I. INTRODUCTION
The physical and geomechanical properties of lunar and Martian surfaces remain an open and fundamentally interesting scientific issue. Understanding the geomechanical characteristics of regolith, including its strength, stiffness, and cohesion, is indispensable for designing successful landing and efficient drilling and coring tools on the lunar and Martian surfaces, optimizing the coring process, and enhancing sample collection methods. It is also essential for evaluating the feasibility and safety of drilling activities, aiding in assessing the landing and drilling site stability.

### B. Preparation of spherical projectiles
A spherical alumina projectile HD-10, with a diameter of 10 mm, was used. Projectiles were dropped from heights of 10.0, 15.0, 20.0, and 25.0 mm. Impact velocities ranged from 0.44 m/s to 0.70 m/s.

## III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

### A. Crater depths
The depths of generated craters increased with the height of the fall. For example, crater depths with fall heights of 10 and 20 mm were:
- **LHS-1:** 5.0 ± 0.7 mm and 6.9 ± 0.8 mm
- **LMS-1:** 3.0 ± 0.2 mm and 3.8 ± 0.3 mm
- **MGS-1:** 4.9 ± 0.9 mm and 6.6 ± 0.8 mm
- **T-8 (Sand):** 2.0 ± 0.3 mm and 3.3 ± 0.7 mm

Notably, crater depths in LHS-1 and MGS-1 were nearly identical and exhibited the greatest depth, while T-8 sand had the smallest depth, indicating the most resistance to penetration.

## IV. CONCLUSIONS
The study highlights significant differences in granular behavior among studied materials. Resistance to crater impact in the lunar highland and Martian regolith layers is lower than in the terrestrial sand or lunar mare layers. This has important implications for landing strategies, drilling efficiency, and foundation stability for construction on the Moon and Mars.
